# 1.0 INTRODUCTION
In descriptive statistics we are describing, presenting, summarising and organising your data, either through numerical calculations or graphs or tables.
 
  $\hspace{2em}$Some of the common measurements in descriptive statistics are central tendency and others the variability of the dataset.

# 1.1 STATISTICS
Statistics is the science, or a branch of mathematics, that concerned with collecting, classifying, analysing, interpreting, and presenting numerical facts and data. Everyone needs to have a basic understanding of statistics, and many college majors require at least one course in statistics. There are three important reasons why statistical literacy is important: 
1) to be informed
2) to understand issues and be able to make sound decision based on data, and
3) to be able to evaluate decisions that affect our life.
Statistics further breaks down into two types: Descriptive and inferential

> [!definition]- Definition:
> Descriptive statistic is the branch of statistics that includes methods for organising and summarising data.
> 
> Inferential statistics is the branch of statistics that involves generalising from a sample to the population from which it was selected and assessing the reliability of such generalisation

# 1.2 TYPES OF DATA
A data set provides information about a group of individuals. These individuals are, typically, representatives chosen from a population under study. Data on the individuals allow us to make inferences about the population.

 $\hspace{2em}$ For example, consider a group of students currently enrolled in a statistics course. One characteristic of the student in the population is the brand of calculator owned (Casio, Hewlett-Packard, Sharp, Texas Instruments, and so on). Another characteristic is the number of textbooks purchased in that semester, and yet another is the distance from the university to each student's permanent residence.
 
 > [!definition]- Definition:
 > Objects are the individuals described by the data.
 
 Variables are characteristics of an individual. There are two types:
  $\hspace{2em}$ Qualitative (categorical) variables partition the individuals into classes.
  $\hspace{2em}$ Quantitative (numerical) variables if each observation is a number.

 $\hspace{2em}$ In previous example, the brand of calculator is a variable, and so are the number of textbooks purchased and distance to the university. The collection of responses from all these students for the calculator brands will forms a categorical data set. The other two attributes, number of textbooks purchased and distance to the university, are both numerical data sets.
 
 $\hspace{2em}$ After the data been summarised, we often wish to draw conclusions or make decisions based on the data. This usually involves generalising from a small group of individuals or objects that we have studied to a much larger group.

 $\hspace{2em}$ For example, the management at a university might be interested in learning why some applicants who were accepted in 2020 failed to enrol at the university. The population of interest to the management consists of all accepted applicants who did not enrol in 2020. Because this population is large and it may be difficult to contact all the individuals, the management might decide to collect data from only 300 selected students. These 300 students constitute a sample. 
 
> [!definition]- Definition
> The entire collection of individuals or objects about which information is desired is called the population of interest.

 A sample is subset of the population, selected for study in some prescribed manner.

## 1.2.2 Levels of Data measurement
The four data measurement levels, from lowest to highest, are nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio.

> [!definition]- Nominal level
> The data can be categorised by labelling them in mutually exclusive groups, but there is no order between the categories
> 
> Example: city of birth, gender, ethnicity, car brands, marital status.

> [!definition]- Ordinal level
> The data can categorize and ||rank|| it in an order, but there is nothing about the ||intervals|| between the rankings.
>
> Example: top 5 Olympic medalists, language ability (beginner, intermediate, fluent) Likert-type questions (very dissatisfied to very satisfied). 

> [!definition]- Interval level
> The data can categorize, rank and has ||equal intervals|| between neighboring data points, but there is ||no true zero point||. 
>
> Example: test scores (IQ or exams), temperature. 
> > [!tip]- can not be expressed in fractions, (fails the double test)

> [!definition]- Ratio level
> The data can categorize, rank, and has equal intervals between neighboring data points, and there is a ||true zero point||. 
>
> Example: height, weight, income in RM.

# 1.3 PICTORIAL AND TABULAR PRESENTATIONS IN DESCRIPTIVE STATISTICS
An appropriate graphical or tabular display of data can be an effective way to summarize and communicate information. When the data set is numerical or categorical, a common way to present the data is in the form of a table, called a  **frequency distribution**.

## 1.3.1 Frequency Distribution
**Frequency** is the number of times the value occurs in the data. The **distribution** of a variable is the pattern of frequencies, meaning the set of all possible values and the frequencies associated with these values.

Frequency distributions are portrayed as frequency tables. Frequency distributions can show either the actual number of observations falling in each range or the percentage of observations. The distribution is called a **relative frequency distribution**. To construct a frequency distribution consisting of ungrouped and grouped data, we will take a look in the examples below.

Before we constructing the frequency distributions table, we need to know several terms that commonly used in describing group frequency distribution:

> [!definition]- Definitions
> * **Class interval**: The interval that includes all the values in a data set bounded by the upper and lower limit of the class.
> * **Upper-class and lower-class limits**: Largest and smallest values of a class interval.
> * **Upper-class and lower-class boundaries**: Midpoint between the upper limit of one class and the lower limit of the next class.
> * **Class width or class size**: Difference between the upper-class boundaries and the lower-class boundaries of a class.
> * **Class midpoint or class mark**: Midpoint of the class interval and is obtained by calculating the mean of the upper-class and lower-class limits.
> * **Relative frequency**: The number of times an event occurs divided by the total number of events occurring in a given scenario.
> * **Percentage distribution**: Express relative frequency in percentage which can be formed by multiplying each relative frequency by 100.
> * **Cumulative frequency distribution**: To determine the number of observations that lie above (or below) a particular value in a data set. The cumulative frequency is calculated by adding each frequency from a frequency distribution table to the sum of its predecessors. The last value will always be equal to the total for all observations. There are two types; less than and more than.

**Ungrouped data**: When the raw data has not been placed in any categories and no summarization has taken placed on the data.
**Grouped data**: When raw data have been grouped into different classes.

## 1.3.2 Histogram


**Histograms** are used to illustrate continuous data but it can also be used to illustrate discrete data. In a histogram, the area of a column represents the frequency of the corresponding class boundaries, so that the area must be proportional to the frequency.

Also, there can be no gaps between the columns in a histogram because the upper boundary of one class is equal to the lower boundary of the neighboring class. A gap can appear only when a class has zero frequency.

## 1.3.4 Cumulative Frequency or Ogive
A **cumulative frequency graph**, or  **ogive** is a line graph displaying the cumulative frequency of each class at its class boundary. The boundaries are marked on the horizontal axis and the cumulative frequencies are marked on the vertical axis.

The two methods of ogives are:
* **Less than ogive**: Graph should start at the lower boundary of the first class (where the cumulative frequency is zero), and end at the upper boundary of the last class. The graph should be increasing from left to right.
> [!graph]- Less than Ogive example
> ![[Pasted image 20260209222059.png]]
* **More than ogive**: Graph should start at the lower boundary of the first class (where the cumulative frequency is the total observations), and end at the upper boundary of the last class. The graph should be decreasing from left to right.

# 1.4 SUMMARY STATISTICS
Visual summaries of data are excellent tools for obtaining preliminary impressions and insights. More formal data analysis often requires the calculation and interpretation of numerical summary measures. That is, from the data we try to extract several summarizing numbers to characterize the data set.

One important characteristic of the data set is its location, and in particular its centre. This section presents methods for describing the location of a data set. In the next section, we will turn to methods for measuring variability in a set of numbers.

## 1.4.1 Measures of Central Tendency
The three common measures of central tendency are;  mean, median and mode.

> [!definition]- Mean
> The **mean** of a given series of values, say, $x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots, x_{n}$ is defined as the sum of these values divided by their total number.
>
> > [!formula] Formula
> > **Ungrouped data**
> > * Population mean: $\mu = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}}{N}$
> > * Sample mean: $\bar{x} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}}{n}$
> >
> > **Grouped data** 
> > * Population mean: $\mu = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}}$
> > * Sample mean: $\bar{x} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}}$
> >
> > **Where:**
> > * $N =$ number of data in the population.
> > * $n =$ number of data in the sample.
> > * $f_{i} =$ number of frequencies in the $i^{th}$ class.
> > * $x_{i} =$ class mark of the $i^{th}$ class.

> [!definition]- Median
> If a set of observations are arranged in ascending order, then the middle most or central value gives the **median**.
>
> If there are $n$ items of data then the median is the value of the $\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{th}$ item.

### Median (Continued)

**Ungrouped data**
* If $n$ is **odd** then there is a middle value and this is the median.
* If $n$ is **even** and the two middle values are $a$ and $b$ then the median is $\frac{a+b}{2}$.

> [!formula]- Formula: Grouped Data Median
> First, locate the median class using $\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{th}$ item. Then, the formula for median is given:
>
> $$Median = L + \left( \frac{\frac{n}{2} - F}{f} \right) \times C$$
>
> **Where:**
> * $n$ is number of observations.
> * $f$ is frequency of the median class.
> * $L$ is lower class boundary for median class.
> * $C$ is size class of the median class.
> * $F$ is cumulative frequency before median class.

### Mode

**Mode** is the value of the variate which occurs most frequently. It represents the most frequent value of a series. In other words, mode is the value of the variable which has the highest frequency.

**Ungrouped data**
In ungrouped data, mode is the value of the variate having the maximum frequency.

> [!formula]- Formula: Grouped Data Mode
> First, locate the mode class by looking the class with highest frequency. Then, the formula for mode is given:
>
> $$Mode = L + \left( \frac{d_1}{d_1 + d_2} \right) \times C$$
>
> **Where:**
> * $L$ is lower class boundary for modal class.
> * $d_1$ is the difference between the frequency of the modal class and the frequency **before** it.

### 1.4.2 Other Types Measures of Central Tendency

The median divides the area under the frequency distribution curve into two equal areas. Using this concept, we can divide the area into more sections:
* **Quartiles ($Q$):** Divide the area into 4 equal parts.
* **Deciles ($D$):** Divide the area into 10 equal parts.
* **Percentiles ($P$):** Divide the area into 100 equal parts.



The **interquartile range (IQR)** is the numerical difference between the upper quartile ($Q_3$) and the lower quartile ($Q_1$), giving the range of the middle half of the values.

#### Ungrouped Data Calculations

> [!formula]- Formula: Percentiles, Quartiles, Deciles (Ungrouped)
> To find the location $j$ for the $k^{th}$ measure:
>
> * **Percentiles:** $j = \frac{k}{100}(n+1)$
> * **Deciles:** $j = \frac{k}{10}(n+1)$
> * **Quartiles:** $Q_1 = P_{25}, Q_2 = P_{50}, Q_3 = P_{75}$
>
> **Rules for $j$:**
> 1. **If $j$ is an integer ($q$):** Value is the $q^{th}$ observation.
> 2. **If $j$ is not an integer ($q + 0.t$):**
>    Value $= q^{th} \text{ observation} + 0.t \times ((q+1)^{th} \text{ obs} - q^{th} \text{ obs})$.

#### Grouped Data Calculations

> [!formula]- Formula: Percentiles, Quartiles, Deciles (Grouped)
> Locate the class using the position term (e.g., $\frac{kn}{100}$), then apply:
>
> * **Percentiles:** $P_k = L_k + \left( \frac{\frac{kn}{100} - F_k}{f_k} \right) \times C_k$
> * **Quartiles:** $Q_k = L_k + \left( \frac{\frac{kn}{4} - F_k}{f_k} \right) \times C_k$
> * **Deciles:** $D_k = L_k + \left( \frac{\frac{kn}{10} - F_k}{f_k} \right) \times C_k$
>

## 1.4.3 Measures of Dispersion
The three common measures of dispersion are variance, standard deviation and range.



> [!definition]- Variance
> Average of the squared deviations about the mean of a set of data.
>
> > [!formula] Formula: Ungrouped Data
> > * **Population variance ($\sigma^2$):**
> >   $$\sigma^2 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}-\mu)^{2}}{N}$$
> > * **Sample variance ($s^2$):**
> >   $$s^2 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-\overline{x})^{2}}{n-1}$$
> >
> > **Where:**
> > * $N =$ number of data in the population.
> > * $n =$ number of data in the sample.
>
> > [!formula] Formula: Grouped Data
> > * **Population variance ($\sigma^2$):**
> >   $$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{N} \left( \sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}x_{i}^{2} - \frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}x_{i})^{2}}{N} \right)$$
> > * **Sample variance ($s^2$):**
> >   $$s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1} \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}x_{i}^{2} - \frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}x_{i})^{2}}{n} \right)$$
> >
> > **Where:**
> > * $f_i$ is number of frequencies in the $i^{th}$ class.
> > * $x_i$ is class mark of the $i^{th}$ class.
> > * $N$ is total frequency in population.
> > * $n$ is total frequency in sample.

> [!definition]- Standard Deviation
> A measure of how dispersed the data is in relation to the mean.
>
> > [!formula] Formula: Ungrouped Data
> > * **Population standard deviation ($\sigma$):**
> >   $$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}-\mu)^{2}}{N}}$$
> > * **Sample standard deviation ($s$):**
> >   $$s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-\overline{x})^{2}}{n-1}}$$
>
> > [!formula] Formula: Grouped Data
> > * **Population standard deviation ($\sigma$):**
> >   $$\sigma = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N} \left( \sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}x_{i}^{2} - \frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}x_{i})^{2}}{N} \right) }$$
> > * **Sample standard deviation ($s$):**
> >   $$s = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{n-1} \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}x_{i}^{2} - \frac{(\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}x_{i})^{2}}{n} \right) }$$

### Coefficient of Variation
The **coefficient of variation** gives us the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean expressed as a percentage. It is useful when comparing the distributions of different means and variances. A distribution with a smaller coefficient of variation than the other is taken as **more consistent** than the other.

> [!formula] Formula
> $$CV = \frac{\text{standard deviation}}{\text{mean}} \times 100\%$$

> [!example]- Example 1.14: Consistency Comparison

## 1.4.4 Skewness
A fundamental task in many statistical analyses is to characterize the location and variability of a data set. A further characterization of the data includes **skewness**.

**Skewness** is a measure of symmetry, or more precisely, the lack of symmetry. A data set is **symmetric** if it looks the same to the left and right of the center point.

The skewness of a distribution can be measured by using **Pearson's coefficient of skewness**.

> [!formula] Formula: Pearson's Coefficient of Skewness ($S_k$)
> $$S_{k} = \frac{3(\text{mean} - \text{median})}{\text{standard deviation}}$$
> OR
> $$S_{k} = \frac{\text{mean} - \text{mode}}{\text{standard deviation}}$$

The value of Pearson's coefficient of skewness and their corresponding type of distribution are given by:
![[Pasted image 20260209225000.png]]
1.  **$S_k > 0$ (Positive Skew):** Skewed (longer tail) to the right.
    * **Relationship:** $\text{mode} < \text{median} < \text{mean}$.
2.  **$S_k = 0$ (Symmetrical Distribution):**
    * **Relationship:** $\text{mode} = \text{median} = \text{mean}$.
3.  **$S_k < 0$ (Negative Skew):** Skewed (longer tail) to the left.
    * **Relationship:** $\text{mean} < \text{median} < \text{mode}$.

> > **Step 1: Calculate Statistics**
> > Midpoints ($x$): 422, 427, 432, 437, 442.

> [!sq]- Question 5: Evaluation Marks
> The following data shows the evaluation marks from a sample of 120 trainee teachers.
>
> | Marks | Frequency |
> | :--- | :--- |
> | 41-50 | 15 |
> | 51-60 | 18 |
> | 61-70 | 33 |
> | 71-80 | 24 |
> | 81-90 | 19 |
> | 91-100 | 11 |
>
> a) Find median.
> b) If the passing mark is 45, determine the percentage of trainees who failed in the evaluation.
> c) If ten percent of the trainees who obtained the highest marks will be given prizes. Determine the minimum mark to receive a prize.
>
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **a) Median**
> > $n=120$. Position $= 60$.
> > Cum Freq: 15, 33, 66 (Median is in 61-70 class).
> > $L=60.5, F=33, f=33, C=10$.
> > $$Median = 60.5 + \frac{60-33}{33} \times 10 = 68.68$$
> >
> > **b) Failed (< 45)**
> > Boundary for 41-50 is 40.5-50.5. Passing mark is 45.
> > We need fraction of first class (40.5 to 50.5) that is below 45.
> > Assuming uniform distribution: $\frac{45-40.5}{10} \times 15 = 6.75$ students.
> > Percentage $= \frac{6.75}{120} \times 100 \approx 5.63\%$.
> >
> > **c) Top 10% Prize**
> > Top 10% means $P_{90}$.
> > $k = \frac{90 \times 120}{100} = 108$.
> > Cum Freq: ... 90 (end of 71-80), 109 (end of 81-90).
> > $P_{90}$ is in 81-90 class ($L=80.5, f=19, F=90, C=10$).
> > $$P_{90} = 80.5 + \frac{108-90}{19} \times 10 = 89.97$$

> [!sq]- Question 6: Skewness from Summary Statistics
> The mean survival times (weeks), $\overline{x}$ of a sample of 20 animals in a clinical trial is 28 with summary statistics $\sum x^{2}=18000$.
> a) Find the standard deviation correct to three decimal places.
> b) It is known that the median is 26, compute Pearson's Coefficient of Skewness. Comment on your answer.
>
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **a) Standard Deviation**
> > Given: $n=20, \bar{x}=28, \sum x^2 = 18000$.
> > Using formula: $s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum x^2 - n(\bar{x})^2}{n-1}}$
> > $$s = \sqrt{\frac{18000 - 20(28)^2}{19}} = \sqrt{\frac{2320}{19}} \approx 11.050$$
> >
> > **b) Skewness**
> > Mean = 28, Median = 26, $s = 11.050$.
> > $$S_k = \frac{3(28 - 26)}{11.050} \approx 0.543$$
> > **Comment:** The distribution is **positively skewed** ($S_k > 0$).
