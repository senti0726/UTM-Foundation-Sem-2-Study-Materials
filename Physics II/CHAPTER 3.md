# 3.2 Electric Current
> [!definition]- Electric Current, $I$
> The rate of flow of charge through a conductor.

> [!formula]- Current Formulas
> > [!formula]- Average Current
> > $I = \frac{Q}{t}$
> 
> > [!formula]- Instantaneous Current
> > $I = \frac{dQ}{dt}$
> 
> > [!unit]- $I$
> > Electric current in Ampere ($A$) or $C\,s^{-1}$
> > > [!definition]- 1 Ampere is defined as
> > > $1\,A = 1\,C/s$ (1 coulomb of charge flowing per second)
> 
> > [!unit]- $Q$
> > Charge passing through a point in $C$
> 
> > [!unit]- $t$
> > Time interval in $s$

> [!compare]- Circuit types
> ![[Pasted image 20260130153615.png]]
> > [!continue]- Complete circuit
> > is one where current can flow all the way around.
> 
> > [!continue]- Open circuit
> > there is a break in the circuit, or we say cut wire / no current flows.

> [!liststart]- Types of Current Flow
> > [!continue]- Direct Current (DC)
> > Charges move around a circuit in the same direction at all times (e.g., produced by a battery).
> 
> > [!continue]- Direction of Flow
> > ![[Pasted image 20260130153649.png]]
> > > [!formula]- Conventional Current
> > > Flows from **Positive (+)** to **Negative (-)** terminal.
> > > (Direction of Electric Field)
> > 
> > > [!formula]- Electron Flow
> > > Flows from **Negative (-)** to **Positive (+)** terminal.
> > > (Electrons accelerate due to electric force)

> [!sq]- Example 3.1: Current is flow of charge
> A steady current of $2.5\,A$ exists in a wire for $4.0\,min$.
> > [!continue]- $a)$ How much total charge $Q$ passed by a given point?
> > $I = 2.5\,A$
> > $t = 4.0\,min = 240\,s$
> > $I = \frac{Q}{t} \implies Q = It$
> > $Q = (2.5)(240)$
> > $\enclose{box}{Q = 600\,C}$
> 
> > [!continue]- $b)$ How many electrons would this be?
> > Charge of one electron $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\,C$
> > $Q = ne$
> > $600 = n(1.6 \times 10^{-19})$
> > $n = \frac{600}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}}$
> > $\enclose{box}{n = 3.75 \times 10^{21}\,\text{electrons}}$

# 3.3 Ohm's Law: Resistance and Resistors

> [!definition]- Ohm's Law
> States that the potential difference across a metallic conductor is proportional to the current flowing through it if its temperature is constant.

> [!formula]- Ohm's Law
> $V \propto I \implies V = IR$
> > [!unit]- $V$
> > Potential difference (Voltage) in $V$
> 
> > [!unit]- $I$
> > Current in $A$
> 
> > [!unit]- $R$
> > Resistance of the conductor in $\Omega$

## Resistance
> [!definition]- Resistance, $R$
> The measure of a component's opposition to the flow of electric charge.
> Defined as the ratio of potential difference to the current.

> [!formula]- Resistance Formula
> $R = \frac{V}{I}$
> > [!unit]- $R$
> > Resistance in Ohm ($\Omega$) or $V\,A^{-1}$
> > > [!info]- If temperature is constant,
> > > $R$ is constant for ohmic conductors.
> 
> > [!unit]- $V$
> > Potential difference in $V$
> 
> > [!unit]- $I$
> > Current in $A$

> [!sq]- Example 3.2: Flashlight bulb resistance
> A small flashlight bulb draws $300\,mA$ from its $1.5\,V$ battery.
> > [!continue]- $a)$ What is the resistance of the bulb?
> > $I = 300\,mA = 300 \times 10^{-3}\,A$
> > $V = 1.5\,V$
> > $R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{1.5}{300 \times 10^{-3}}$
> > $\enclose{box}{R = 5\,\Omega}$
> 
> > [!continue]- $b)$ If the battery becomes weak and voltage drops to $1.2\,V$, how would the current change?
> > Assuming $R$ stays constant ($5\,\Omega$):
> > $V = 1.2\,V$
> > $I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{1.2}{5}$
> > $\enclose{box}{I = 0.24\,A}$

# 3.4 Resistivity

> [!definition]- Resistivity, $\rho$
> The resistance of a unit cross-sectional area per unit length of the material.
> It is a measure of a material's ability to oppose the flow of an electric current.

> [!formula]- Resistivity Formula
> $R = \frac{\rho l}{A} \implies \rho = \frac{RA}{l}$
> > [!unit]- $\rho$
> > Resistivity in $\Omega\cdot m$
> 
> > [!unit]- $R$
> > Resistance in $\Omega$
> 
> > [!unit]- $A$
> > Cross-sectional area in $m^2$ (For wire, $A = \pi r^2$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $l$
> > Length of the material in $m$

> [!liststart]- Characteristics
> > [!continue]- Resistivity depends on
> > > [!continue]- the type of the material
> > 
> > > [!continue]- the temperature
> 
> > [!continue]- A good electric conductor
> > have very low resistivities
> 
> > [!continue]- A good insulators
> > have very high resistivities

> [!table]- Resistivity and Temperature Coefficients (at $20^\circ C$)
> | Material | Resistivity $\rho\,(\Omega\cdot m)$ | Temp. Coeff. $\alpha\,(C^\circ)^{-1}$ |
> | :--- | :--- | :--- |
> | **Conductors** | | |
> | Silver | $1.59 \times 10^{-8}$ | $0.0061$ |
> | Copper | $1.68 \times 10^{-8}$ | $0.0068$ |
> | Gold | $2.44 \times 10^{-8}$ | $0.0034$ |
> | Aluminum | $2.65 \times 10^{-8}$ | $0.00429$ |
> | Tungsten | $5.60 \times 10^{-8}$ | $0.0045$ |
> | Iron | $9.71 \times 10^{-8}$ | $0.00651$ |
> | Platinum | $10.60 \times 10^{-8}$ | $0.003927$ |
> | Mercury | $98.00 \times 10^{-8}$ | $0.0009$ |
> | Nichrome | $100.00 \times 10^{-8}$ | $0.0004$ |
> | **Semiconductors** | | |
> | Carbon (graphite) | $(3-60) \times 10^{-5}$ | $-0.0005$ |
> | Germanium | $(1-500) \times 10^{-3}$ | $-0.05$ |
> | Silicon | $0.1-60$ | $-0.07$ |
> | **Insulators** | | |
> | Glass | $10^9 - 10^{12}$ | - |
> | Hard rubber | $10^{13} - 10^{15}$ | - |

> [!sq]- Example 3: Resistivity Calculation
> The resistivity of a silver wire with a radius of $5.04 \times 10^{-4}\,m$ is $1.59 \times 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot m$. If the length of the wire is $3.0\,m$, what is the resistance?
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > $r = 5.04 \times 10^{-4}\,m$
> > $\rho = 1.59 \times 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot m$
> > $l = 3.0\,m$
> >
> > Calculate Area $A$:
> > $A = \pi r^2 = \pi (5.04 \times 10^{-4})^2 = 7.98 \times 10^{-7}\,m^2$
> >
> > Calculate Resistance $R$:
> > $R = \frac{\rho l}{A} = \frac{(1.59 \times 10^{-8})(3.0)}{7.98 \times 10^{-7}}$
> > $\enclose{box}{R = 5.98 \times 10^{-2}\,\Omega}$

> [!sq]- Example 3.3: Speaker Wires
> Connect stereo to remote speakers.
> > [!continue]- $a)$ If each wire is $20\,m$ long, what diameter $2r$ copper wire should be used to keep resistance less than $0.10\,\Omega$ per wire?
> > $l = 20\,m$, $R = 0.10\,\Omega$
> > Copper $\rho = 1.68 \times 10^{-8}\,\Omega\cdot m$
> > $R = \frac{\rho l}{A} \implies A = \frac{\rho l}{R}$
> > $A = \frac{(1.68 \times 10^{-8})(20)}{0.10} = 3.36 \times 10^{-6}\,m^2$
> >
> > Find radius $r$:
> > $A = \pi r^2 \implies r = \sqrt{\frac{A}{\pi}}$
> > $r = \sqrt{\frac{3.36 \times 10^{-6}}{\pi}} = 1.034 \times 10^{-3}\,m$
> >
> > Find diameter $d$:
> > $d = 2r = 2(1.034 \times 10^{-3})$
> > $\enclose{box}{d = 2.07 \times 10^{-3}\,m \text{ or } 2.07\,mm}$
> 
> > [!continue]- $b)$ If current to each speaker is $4.0\,A$, what is the voltage drop across each wire?
> > $I = 4.0\,A$, $R = 0.10\,\Omega$
> > $V = IR = (4.0)(0.10)$
> > $\enclose{box}{V = 0.4\,V}$

## Temperature Dependence
> [!continue]- For any given material
> The resistivity increases with temperature.

> [!formula]- Temperature Dependence Formula
> $\rho_T = \rho_0 [1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]$
> $R_T = R_0 [1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]$
> > [!unit]- $\rho_T$ / $R_T$
> > Resistivity/Resistance at temperature $T$
> 
> > [!unit]- $\rho_0$ / $R_0$
> > Resistivity/Resistance at reference temperature $T_0$ (usually $20^\circ C$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $\alpha$
> > Temperature Coefficient of resistivity in $(C^\circ)^{-1}$

> [!sq]- Example 3.4: Resistance Thermometer
> At $20.0^\circ C$, the resistance of a platinum thermometer is $164.2\,\Omega$. When placed in a solution, resistance is $187.4\,\Omega$. What is the temperature of the solution?
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > $T_0 = 20^\circ C$, $R_0 = 164.2\,\Omega$
> > $R_T = 187.4\,\Omega$
> > From table, Platinum $\alpha = 0.003927\,(C^\circ)^{-1}$
> >
> > $R_T = R_0 [1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]$
> > $187.4 = 164.2 [1 + 0.003927(T - 20)]$
> > $\frac{187.4}{164.2} = 1 + 0.003927(T - 20)$
> > $1.1413 = 1 + 0.003927(T - 20)$
> > $0.1413 = 0.003927(T - 20)$
> > $T - 20 = \frac{0.1413}{0.003927} \approx 36$
> > $T = 36 + 20$
> > $\enclose{box}{T = 56^\circ C}$

# 3.5 Electric Power

> [!definition]- Electric Power, $P$
> The energy transformed by a device per unit time.

> [!formula]- Power Formula
> $P = \frac{dU}{dt} = \frac{dq}{dt}V = IV$
> > [!continue]- For Ohmic devices ($V=IR$)
> > Substitute $V=IR \implies P = I(IR) = I^2 R$
> > Substitute $I=V/R \implies P = (\frac{V}{R})V = \frac{V^2}{R}$
> >
> > $\enclose{box}{P = IV = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}}$
> 
> > [!unit]- $P$
> > Power in Watt ($W$) or $J\,s^{-1}$

> [!sq]- Example 3.5: Headlights
> Calculate the resistance of a $40\text{-}W$ automobile headlight designed for $12\,V$.
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > $P = 40\,W$, $V = 12\,V$
> > Using $P = \frac{V^2}{R} \implies R = \frac{V^2}{P}$
> > $R = \frac{12^2}{40} = \frac{144}{40}$
> > $\enclose{box}{R = 3.6\,\Omega}$

## Energy Consumption
> [!continue]- What you pay for on your electric bill
> is not power, but $\color{orange}i$ - the power consumption multiplied by $\color{orange}ii$.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > energy
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > time

> [!unit]- Unit of Commercial Energy
> Kilowatt-hour ($kWh$)
> > [!unit]- Conversion to Joules
> > $1\,kWh = (1000\,W)(3600\,s)$
> > $1\,kWh = 3.60 \times 10^6\,J$

> [!sq]- Example 3.6: Electric heater
> An electric heater draws a steady $15.0\,A$ on a $120\text{-}V$ line.
> > [!continue]- $a)$ How much power does it require?
> > $P = IV = (15.0)(120)$
> > $\enclose{box}{P = 1800\,W = 1.8\,kW}$
> 
> > [!continue]- $b)$ How much does it cost per month (30 days) if it operates $3.0\,h$ per day at $9.2$ cents per $kWh$?
> > Total time $t = 30 \text{ days} \times 3.0\,h/\text{day} = 90\,h$
> > Energy $E = Pt = (1.8\,kW)(90\,h) = 162\,kWh$
> > Cost $= 162 \times \$0.092$
> > $\enclose{box}{\text{Cost} = \$14.90}$

> [!sq]- Example 3.7: Lightning bolt
> Lightning transfers $10^9\,J$ of energy across a potential difference of $5 \times 10^7\,V$ during a time interval of $0.2\,s$.
> > [!continue]- $a)$ Total amount of charge transferred.
> > $U = qV \implies q = \frac{U}{V}$
> > $q = \frac{10^9}{5 \times 10^7}$
> > $\enclose{box}{q = 20\,C}$
> 
> > [!continue]- $b)$ The current in the lightning bolt.
> > $I = \frac{q}{t} = \frac{20}{0.2}$
> > $\enclose{box}{I = 100\,A}$
> 
> > [!continue]- $c)$ Average power delivered.
> > $P = IV = (100)(5 \times 10^7)$
> > $\enclose{box}{P = 5 \times 10^9\,W}$

# 3.6 Alternating Current (AC)
> [!formula]- Power in AC
> Multiplying current and voltage gives power, which varies with time.
> $P = I^2 R = I_0^2 R \sin^2 \omega t$
> > [!picture]- Power vs Time Graph
> > ![[Pasted image 20260131134250.png]]
>
> > [!unit]- $P$
> > Instantaneous power in $W$
>
> > [!unit]- $I_0$
> > Peak current in $A$
>
> > [!unit]- $R$
> > Resistance in $\Omega$
>
> > [!unit]- $\omega$
> > Angular frequency in $rad\,s^{-1}$
>
> > [!unit]- $t$
> > Time in $s$

> [!formula]- Average Power, $\bar{P}$
> Since the average of $\sin^2 \omega t$ is $1/2$:
> $\bar{P} = \frac{1}{2}I_0^2 R$
> $\bar{P} = I_{rms}^2 R = \frac{V_{rms}^2}{R} = I_{rms}V_{rms}$
> > [!unit]- $\bar{P}$
> > Average power in $W$
>
> > [!unit]- $I_{rms}$
> > Root-mean-square current in $A$
>
> > [!unit]- $V_{rms}$
> > Root-mean-square voltage in $V$
>
> > [!unit]- $R$
> > Resistance in $\Omega$
## Root-Mean-Square (RMS) Values
> [!continue]- Definition
> The current and voltage both have average values of zero, so we use RMS values to express effective AC magnitude.

> [!formula]- RMS Formulas
> $I_{rms} = \sqrt{\bar{I^2}} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}} = 0.707 I_0$
> $V_{rms} = \sqrt{\bar{V^2}} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}} = 0.707 V_0$
> > [!unit]- $I_0, V_0$
> > Peak (maximum) current and voltage
> 
> > [!unit]- $I_{rms}, V_{rms}$
> > Effective current and voltage

> [!sq]- Example 3.8: Hair dryer
> A $1000\text{-}W$ hair dryer is connected to a $120\text{-}V$ line (RMS).
> ![[Pasted image 20260131134825.png]]
> > [!continue]- $a)$ Calculate the resistance and the peak current.
> > **Find Resistance:**
> > $P = \frac{V_{rms}^2}{R} \implies R = \frac{V_{rms}^2}{P}$
> > $R = \frac{120^2}{1000} = \frac{14400}{1000}$
> > $\enclose{box}{R = 14.4\,\Omega}$
> >
> > **Find RMS Current:**
> > $P = I_{rms} V_{rms} \implies I_{rms} = \frac{P}{V_{rms}}$
> > $I_{rms} = \frac{1000}{120} = 8.33\,A$
> >
> > **Find Peak Current:**
> > $I_{rms} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}} \implies I_0 = I_{rms}\sqrt{2}$
> > $I_0 = 8.33(\sqrt{2})$
> > $\enclose{box}{I_0 = 11.8\,A}$
> 
> > [!continue]- $b)$ What happens if it is connected to a $240\text{-}V$ line in Britain?
> > Assuming resistance $R$ stays constant ($14.4\,\Omega$):
> > New Power $\bar{P} = \frac{V_{rms}^2}{R}$
> > $\bar{P} = \frac{240^2}{14.4} = \frac{57600}{14.4}$
> > $\enclose{box}{\bar{P} = 4000\,W}$
> > (The dryer would likely burn out).
