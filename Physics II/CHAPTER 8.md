# 8.2 Reflection: Formation of Image by Flat and Spherical Mirrors

> [!definition]- Reflection of Light
> The return of all or part of a beam of light when it encounters the boundary between two media.

> [!liststart]- Law of Reflection
> ![[Pasted image 20260202220002.png]]
> > [!continue]- The angle of reflection $\theta_r$
> > equals the angle of incidence $\theta_i$.
> > $\theta_i = \theta_r$
> 
> > [!continue]- Normal
> > The angles are measured with respect to the **normal** (a line perpendicular to the surface).

## Plane Mirrors
> [!continue]- Image Formation
> When you look into a flat mirror, you see an image that appears to be $\color{orange}i$ the mirror. This is called a $\color{orange}ii$ because light does not actually pass through the image position.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > behind
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > virtual image

> [!liststart]- Properties of Image in Plane Mirror
> ![[Pasted image 20260202215817.png]]
> > [!continue]- Type
> > Virtual (cannot be formed on a screen).
> 
> > [!continue]- Orientation
> > Upright.
> 
> > [!continue]- Size
> > Same size as the object ($h_i = h_o$).
> 
> > [!continue]- Distance
> > The image distance ($d_i$) equals the object distance ($d_o$). The image is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.

> [!liststart]- Reflection varying with surface type
> > [!continue]- Rough surface
> > when light reflects from a rough surface, the law of reflection still holds, but the angle of incidence varies. your eyes sees reflected light at all angles. This is called ||diffuse reflection||
> 
> > [!continue]- Smooth surface (from mirror)
> > your eyes must be in correct position. This is called ||specular reflection||
## Spherical Mirrors
> [!liststart]- Types of Spherical Mirrors
> > [!continue]- Concave Mirror
> > ![[Pasted image 20260202220140.png]]
> > The reflecting surface is on the **inner** curve (like a cave).
> > > [!info]- property of focal point
> > > real (principal)
> 
> > [!continue]- Convex Mirror
> > ![[Pasted image 20260202220128.png]]
> > The reflecting surface is on the **outer** curve.
> > > [!info]- property of focal point
> > > virtual

> [!definition]- Focal Point (Focus), $F$
> > [!continue]- For concave mirror
> > a point where the incident parallel rays converge after reflection on the mirror.
> 
> > [!continue]- For convex mirror
> > a point where the incident parallel rays seem to diverge from a point behind the mirror after reflection

> [!definition]- Focal length
> the distance between the focal point (focus) $F$ and pole$P$ of the spherical mirror.

> [!formula]- Focal Length, $f$
> $f = \frac{r}{2}$
> > [!unit]- $f$
> > Focal length in meters ($m$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $r$
> > Radius of curvature of the mirror in meters ($m$)

> [!definition]- Paraxial rays
> the rays that are near to and almost parallel to the principle axis.

> [!continue]- If the curvature of the mirror is large
> parallel rays striking a spherical mirror do not all converge (focus) at exactly the same place. 
> > [!info]- This is called
> > spherical aberration
> > > [!note]- Spherical aberration can be avoided by using
> > > parabolic reflector; these are more difficult and expensive to make and so are used only when necessary, such as in ||research telescopes||

> [!continue]- If the curvature is small
> the focus is much more precise; the focal point is where rays converge.
> ![[Pasted image 20260202221339.png]]

> [!liststart]- Ray Tracing Rules (for finding images)
> > [!continue]- Ray 1
> > ![[Pasted image 20260202221625.png]]
> > Leaves the object **parallel** to the principal axis $\rightarrow$ reflects through the **focal point** $F$.
> 
> > [!continue]- Ray 2
> > ![[Pasted image 20260202221651.png]]
> > Leaves the object and passes through the **focal point** $F$ $\rightarrow$ reflects **parallel** to the principal axis.
> 
> > [!continue]- Ray 3
> > ![[Pasted image 20260202221721.png]]
> > Leaves the object and passes through the **center of curvature** $C$ $\rightarrow$ reflects back along the **same path** (since it hits the mirror normally).

## Mirror Equation and Magnification
> [!liststart]- Characteristic of image produced by different type of mirrors
> > [!continue]- Concave mirror
> > ![[Pasted image 20260202222420.png]]
> > compared to object,
> > > [!continue]- $d_{0}>C$
> > > Real, inverted, diminished
> > 
> > > [!continue]- $d_{0}=C$
> > > Real, inverted, same size
> > 
> > > [!continue]- $C<d_{0}<F$
> > > Real, inverted, magnified
> > 
> > > [!continue]- $d_{0}=F$
> > > Virtual, upright, magnified
> > 
> > > [!continue]- $d_{0}<F$
> > > Virtual, upright, magnified
> > 
> > > [!liststart]- The pattern found in different property
> > > > [!tip]- the image becomes virtual when $d_{0}\leq F$
> > > 
> > > > [!tip]- the image is inverted if real and upright if virtual
> > > 
> > > > [!tip]- the image magnifies/same size/diminishes as $C$ is greater/same/less than $d_{0}$
> 
> > [!continue]- Convex mirror
> > ![[Pasted image 20260202222729.png]]
> > always virtual, upright, diminished
> > > [!example]- real world application
> > > driving mirror
> > > > [!because]- used because
> > > > it has a wide field of view and provide an upright image.

> [!formula]- Mirror Equation
> Relates object distance, image distance, and focal length.
> ![[Pasted image 20260202222420.png]]
> $\frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{f}$
> > [!unit]- $d_o$
> > Object distance (distance from object to mirror) in $m$
> 
> > [!unit]- $d_i$
> > Image distance (distance from image to mirror) in $m$
> 
> > [!unit]- $f$
> > Focal length in $m$

> [!formula]- Magnification, $m$
> Relates the height of the image to the height of the object.
> $m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = -\frac{d_i}{d_o}$
> > [!unit]- $m$
> > Magnification (dimensionless ratio)
> 
> > [!unit]- $h_i$
> > Image height in $m$
> 
> > [!unit]- $h_o$
> > Object height in $m$
> 
> > [!unit]- $d_i$
> > Image distance in $m$
> 
> > [!unit]- $d_o$
> > Object distance in $m$
> 
> > [!unit]- $-$
> > indicates that the image is inverted.

> [!liststart]- Sign Conventions
> > [!continue]- Focal Length ($f$)
> > * **Concave Mirror:** Positive ($+$)
> > * **Convex Mirror:** Negative ($-$)
> 
> > [!continue]- Image Distance ($d_i$)
> > * **Real Image (in front of mirror):** Positive ($+$)
> > * **Virtual Image (behind mirror):** Negative ($-$)
> 
> > [!continue]- Magnification ($m$)
> > * **Upright Image:** Positive ($+$)
> > * **Inverted Image:** Negative ($-$)
> 
> > [!note]- Real image properties
> >  - Real image is formed by ||the actual light rays that pass through the image||
> >  - Real image can be ||projected on the screen||

> [!sq]- Example 8-1: Image in a concave mirror
> A $1.50\text{-}cm\text{-high}$ diamond ring is placed $20.0\,cm$ from a concave mirror with a radius of curvature of $30.0\,cm$. Determine:
> > [!continue]- $a)$ The position of the image ($d_i$).
> > **Given:**
> > $h_o = 1.50\,cm$
> > $d_o = 20.0\,cm$
> > $r = 30.0\,cm$ (Concave $\rightarrow r > 0$)
> >
> > **Step 1: Find Focal Length**
> > $f = \frac{r}{2} = \frac{30.0}{2} = 15.0\,cm$
> >
> > **Step 2: Use Mirror Equation**
> > $\frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{f}$
> > $\frac{1}{20.0} + \frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{15.0}$
> > $\frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{15.0} - \frac{1}{20.0}$
> > $\frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{4 - 3}{60.0} = \frac{1}{60.0}$
> > $\enclose{box}{d_i = 60.0\,cm}$
> > (Positive $d_i$ means the image is Real).
>
> > [!continue]- $b)$ The size of the image ($h_i$).
> > **Use Magnification Equation**
> > $m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = -\frac{d_i}{d_o}$
> > $h_i = -h_o \left( \frac{d_i}{d_o} \right)$
> > $h_i = -1.50 \left( \frac{60.0}{20.0} \right)$
> > $h_i = -1.50 (3.0)$
> > $\enclose{box}{h_i = -4.50\,cm}$
> > (Negative height means the image is Inverted).

> [!sq]- Example 8-2: Object closer to concave mirror
> What if the object in Example 8-1 is placed $10.0\,cm$ from the mirror (inside the focal point)?
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **Given:**
> > $d_o = 10.0\,cm$
> > $f = 15.0\,cm$
> >
> > **Calculate Image Distance:**
> > $\frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{d_o}$
> > $\frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{15.0} - \frac{1}{10.0}$
> > $\frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{2 - 3}{30.0} = -\frac{1}{30.0}$
> > $\enclose{box}{d_i = -30.0\,cm}$
> > (Negative $d_i$ means the image is Virtual and behind the mirror).
> >
> > **Calculate Magnification:**
> > $m = -\frac{d_i}{d_o} = -\frac{-30.0}{10.0} = +3.0$
> > (Positive $m$ means the image is Upright and larger).

> [!sq]- Example 8-3: Convex rearview mirror
> An external rearview mirror on a car is a convex mirror with a radius of curvature of $16.0\,m$. Determine the location of the image and its magnification for a bus located $10.0\,m$ from the mirror.
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **Given:**
> > $r = -16.0\,m$ (Convex mirror $\rightarrow$ negative radius)
> > $d_o = 10.0\,m$
> >
> > **Step 1: Find Focal Length**
> > $f = \frac{r}{2} = \frac{-16.0}{2} = -8.0\,m$
> >
> > **Step 2: Find Image Distance**
> > $\frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{d_o}$
> > $\frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{-8.0} - \frac{1}{10.0}$
> > $\frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{-5 - 4}{40.0} = -\frac{9}{40.0}$
> > $d_i = -\frac{40.0}{9}$
> > $\enclose{box}{d_i \approx -4.44\,m}$
> > (Virtual image behind the mirror).
> >
> > **Step 3: Find Magnification**
> > $m = -\frac{d_i}{d_o} = -\frac{-4.44}{10.0}$
> > $\enclose{box}{m = +0.44}$
> > (Upright and diminished).

# 8.3 Refraction

> [!definition]- Refraction
> The changing of direction of a light ray and its speed of propagation as it passes from one medium into another.

## 8.3.1 Index of Refraction

> [!definition]- Refractive Index (Index of Refraction), $n$
> Defined as the constant ratio $\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}$ for the two given media.
> > [!continue]- Characteristics
> > * The value depends on the $\color{orange}i$ and the $\color{orange}ii$.
> > * It is $\color{orange}iii$ and its value is always $\color{orange}iv$.
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > > type of medium
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > > colour of the light
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iii$
> > > dimensionless
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iv$
> > > greater than 1

> [!formula]- Relative Refractive Index
> Consider a light ray traveling from Medium 1 into Medium 2.
> $_{1}n_{2} = \frac{\text{velocity of light in medium 1}}{\text{velocity of light in medium 2}} = \frac{v_1}{v_2}$
> > [!unit]- $n_2$
> > Refractive index of medium 2 relative to medium 1
> 
> > [!unit]- $v_1$
> > Velocity of light in medium 1 (containing incident ray) in $m\,s^{-1}$
> 
> > [!unit]- $v_2$
> > Velocity of light in medium 2 (containing refracted ray) in $m\,s^{-1}$

> [!formula]- Absolute Refractive Index, $n$
> For incident ray traveling from vacuum or air into the medium.
> $n = \frac{\text{velocity of light in vacuum}}{\text{velocity of light in medium}} = \frac{c}{v}$
> > [!unit]- $n$
> > Absolute refractive index
> 
> > [!unit]- $c$
> > Speed of light in vacuum ($3.00 \times 10^8\,m\,s^{-1}$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $v$
> > Speed of light in the medium in $m\,s^{-1}$

> [!table]- Indices of Refraction ($n = c/v$)
> | Material | $n$ |
> | :--- | :--- |
> | Vacuum | $1.0000$ |
> | Air (at STP) | $1.0003$ |
> | Water | $1.33$ |
> | Ethyl Alcohol | $1.36$ |
> | **Glass** | |
> | Fused quartz | $1.46$ |
> | Crown glass | $1.52$ |
> | Light flint | $1.58$ |
> | Lucite or Plexiglas | $1.51$ |
> | Sodium chloride | $1.53$ |
> | Diamond | $2.42$ |
> > [!info]- Note
> > Values are for light with wavelength $\lambda = 589\,nm$.
## 8.3.2 Snell's Law

> [!liststart]- Laws of Refraction
> > [!continue]- Plane
> > The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal all lie in the ||same plane||.
> 
> > [!continue]- Snell's Law
> > For two given media, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant.
> > $\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \text{constant}$

> [!formula]- Snell's Law Equation
> $n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$
> > [!unit]- $n_1$
> > Refractive index of medium 1 (containing incident ray)
> 
> > [!unit]- $\theta_1$ (or $i$)
> > Angle of incidence (angle between incident ray and normal)
> 
> > [!unit]- $n_2$
> > Refractive index of medium 2 (containing refracted ray)
> 
> > [!unit]- $\theta_2$ (or $r$)
> > Angle of refraction (angle between refracted ray and normal)

> [!liststart]- Refraction Behavior
> > [!continue]- Case 1: Less Dense to Denser ($n_1 < n_2$)
> > ![[Pasted image 20260203025606.png]]
> > Light ray bends ||toward the normal||.
> > Thus, $r < i$.
> > 
> 
> > [!continue]- Case 2: Denser to Less Dense ($n_1 > n_2$)
> > ![[Pasted image 20260203025640.png]]
> > Light ray bends ||away from the normal||.
> > Thus, $r > i$.

> [!sq]- Example 8-6: Refraction through flat glass
> Light traveling in air strikes a flat piece of uniformly thick glass at an incident angle of $60^{\circ}$. If the index of refraction of the glass is 1.50:
> ![[Pasted image 20260203030218.png]]
> > [!continue]- $a)$ What is the angle of refraction $\theta_A$ in the glass?
> > **Given:**
> > $n_{air} = 1.00$
> > $n_{glass} = 1.50$
> > $\theta_{incident} = 60^{\circ}$
> >
> > **Apply Snell's Law:**
> > $n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin \theta_2$
> > $(1.00) \sin 60^{\circ} = (1.50) \sin \theta_A$
> > $\sin \theta_A = \frac{\sin 60^{\circ}}{1.50} = \frac{0.866}{1.50} = 0.577$
> > $\theta_A = \sin^{-1}(0.577)$
> > $\enclose{box}{\theta_A = 35.3^{\circ}}$
>
> > [!continue]- $b)$ What is the angle $\theta_B$ at which the ray emerges from the glass?
> > **Geometric Analysis:**
> > The surfaces are parallel, so the angle of incidence at the second interface is $\theta_A = 35.3^{\circ}$ (alternate interior angles).
> >
> > **Apply Snell's Law again:**
> > $n_{glass} \sin \theta_A = n_{air} \sin \theta_B$
> > $(1.50) \sin 35.3^{\circ} = (1.00) \sin \theta_B$
> > $(1.50)(0.577) = \sin \theta_B$
> > $0.866 = \sin \theta_B$
> > $\theta_B = \sin^{-1}(0.866)$
> > $\enclose{box}{\theta_B = 60^{\circ}}$
> > (The emerging ray is parallel to the incident ray).

## Apparent Depth

> [!continue]- Phenomenon
> When you look at an object underwater, it appears to be $\color{orange}i$ than it actually is due to $\color{orange}ii$.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > shallower (closer to the surface)
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > refraction of light

> [!formula]- Apparent Depth Formula
> For viewing from air (rarefied medium) into water/glass (denser medium):
> $n = \frac{\text{Real Depth}}{\text{Apparent Depth}} = \frac{H}{h}$
> > [!unit]- $n$
> > Refractive index of the denser medium (e.g., water)
> 
> > [!unit]- $H$
> > Real depth in $m$
> 
> > [!unit]- $h$
> > Apparent depth in $m$

> [!sq]- Example 8-7: Apparent depth of a pool
> A swimmer has dropped her goggles to the bottom of a pool at the shallow end, marked as $1.0\,m$ deep. The goggles don't look that deep. How deep do the goggles appear to be when you look straight down into the water? ($n_{water} = 1.33$)
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **Given:**
> > Real depth $H = 1.0\,m$
> > $n_{water} = 1.33$
> >
> > **Calculate Apparent Depth:**
> > $n = \frac{H}{h}$
> > $1.33 = \frac{1.0}{h}$
> > $h = \frac{1.0}{1.33}$
> > $\enclose{box}{h = 0.75\,m}$
> >
> > **Reasoning:**
> > When light rays from the goggles travel from water to air, light is refracted ||away from the normal||. This causes the image of the goggles to appear ||closer to the surface|| of the water as seen by the observer.
