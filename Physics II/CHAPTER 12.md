# 12.1 Structure and Properties of the Nucleus

> [!continue]- Composition of the Nucleus
> The nucleus is made of $\color{orange}i$ and $\color{orange}ii$, collectively called $\color{orange}iii$.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > protons
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > neutrons
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iii$
> > nucleons

> [!table]- Properties of Nucleons
> | Particle | Charge (C) | Mass (kg) | Mass (u) | Mass ($MeV/c^2$) |
> | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
> | **Proton (p)** | $+1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ | $1.67262 \times 10^{-27}$ | $1.007276$ | $938.27$ | 
> | **Neutron (n)** | $0$ (neutral) | $1.67493 \times 10^{-27}$ | $1.008665$ | $939.57$ | 
> | **Electron (e)** | $-1.60 \times 10^{-19}$ | $9.1094 \times 10^{-31}$ | $0.00054858$ | $0.51100$ | 

> [!formula]- Notation for Nuclides
> A specific nucleus is called a ||nuclide|| and is represented as:
> ${}_{Z}^{A}X$
> * **X**: ||Chemical symbol for the element|| 
> * **Z**: ||Atomic number (Number of protons)||
> * **A**: ||Atomic mass number (Total number of nucleons)||
> * **N**: ||Neutron number ($N = A - Z$) ||

> [!sq]- Example 12-1: Sodium Atom
> For the nuclide ${}_{11}^{23}Na$:
> * Atomic Number ($Z$) = ||11|| 
> * Atomic Mass ($A$) = ||23|| 
> * Number of Protons = ||11||
> * Number of Electrons = ||11|| (in a neutral atom) 
> * Number of Neutrons ($N$) = $23 - 11 =$ ||12|| 

> [!definition]- Isotopes
> Nuclides that have the same ||atomic number ($Z$)|| (same element) but different ||mass number ($A$)||.
> * This means they have the same number of ||protons|| but different number of ||neutrons||. 
> 
> **Examples:**
> * **Hydrogen:** ${}_{1}^{1}H$ (Protium), ${}_{1}^{2}H$ (Deuterium), ${}_{1}^{3}H$ (Tritium) 
> * **Oxygen:** ${}_{8}^{16}O$, ${}_{8}^{17}O$, ${}_{8}^{18}O$ 
>  
> > [!info]- Natural abundance is
> > the percentage of a particular element that consists of a particular isotope in nature

> [!formula]- Nuclear Radius, $r$
> The size of the nucleus is approximate due to wave-particle duality.
> $r \approx (1.2 \times 10^{-15}\,m)(A^{1/3})$
> > [!unit]- $r$
> > Radius of the nucleus in meters ($m$) 
> 
> > [!unit]- $A$
> > Atomic mass number (dimensionless) 
> 
> > [!info]- Scale Comparison
> > * Atom radius: $\approx 10^{-10}\,m$
> > * Nucleus radius: $\approx 10^{-15}\,m$ 

> [!definition]- Unified Atomic Mass Unit ($u$)
> Defined as 1/12th the mass of a Carbon-12 atom.
> $1\,u = 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\,kg = 931.5\,MeV/c^2$

# 12.2 Binding Energy and Nuclear Forces

> [!continue]- Mass Defect
> The total mass of a stable nucleus is always $\color{orange}i$ than the sum of the masses of its separate protons and neutrons.
> * The missing mass has become $\color{orange}ii$ released during the formation of the nucleus.
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > less
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > energy

> [!definition]- Mass defect
> mass difference between the total mass of the constituent nucleons and the mass of a nucleus.

> [!formula]- Mass Defect, $\Delta m$
> The difference between the total mass of the constituent nucleons and the mass of the nucleus.
> $\Delta m = (Z m_p + N m_n) - M_A$
> > [!unit]- $\Delta m$
> > Mass defect in atomic mass units ($u$) or $kg$
> 
> > [!unit]- $Z$
> > Atomic number (number of protons)
> 
> > [!unit]- $m_p$
> > Mass of a proton ($1.007276\,u$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $N$
> > Neutron number (number of neutrons)
> 
> > [!unit]- $m_n$
> > Mass of a neutron ($1.008665\,u$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $M_A$
> > Mass of the nucleus in atomic mass units ($u$)

> [!definition]- Binding Energy
> The energy required to separate completely all the nucleons in the nucleus.
> * It is the energy equivalent of the ||mass defect||.

> [!formula]- Binding Energy Equation
> $E_B = (\Delta m)c^2$
> > [!formula]- Practical Calculation (using u)
> > Since $1\,u = 931.5\,MeV/c^2$:
> > $E_B (\text{in } MeV) = \Delta m (\text{in } u) \times 931.5$
> 
> > [!unit]- $E_B$
> > Binding energy in Joules ($J$) or Mega-electron-volts ($MeV$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $\Delta m$
> > Mass defect in $kg$ or $u$
> 
> > [!unit]- $c$
> > Speed of light ($3.00 \times 10^8\,m\,s^{-1}$)

> [!sq]- Example 12-2: Helium Mass Defect
> Compare the mass of a Helium atom (${}_2^4He$) to the total mass of its constituent particles. ($M_{He} = 4.002603\,u$).
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **Given:**
> > $Z = 2$ (protons), $N = 4 - 2 = 2$ (neutrons).
> > $m_p = 1.007825\,u$ (using H atom mass to account for electrons usually, or bare proton mass. Slide uses 1.007825 which includes electron).
> > $m_n = 1.008665\,u$.
> >
> > **Calculation:**
> > $\Delta m = (2 m_p + 2 m_n) - M_{He}$
> > $\Delta m = [2(1.007825) + 2(1.008665)] - 4.002603$
> > $\Delta m = [2.01565 + 2.01733] - 4.002603$
> > $\Delta m = 4.03298 - 4.002603$
> > $\enclose{box}{\Delta m = 0.030378\,u}$

> [!sq]- Example 2: Binding Energy of Aluminum
> Calculate the binding energy of an Aluminum nucleus (${}_{13}^{27}Al$) in $MeV$.
> ($M_{Al} = 26.98154\,u$, $m_n = 1.00867\,u$, $m_p = 1.00782\,u$).
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **Given:**
> > $A = 27$, $Z = 13$, $N = 27 - 13 = 14$.
> >
> > **Step 1: Calculate Mass Defect ($\Delta m$)**
> > $\Delta m = [13(1.00782) + 14(1.00867)] - 26.98154$
> > $\Delta m = [13.10166 + 14.12138] - 26.98154$
> > $\Delta m = 27.22304 - 26.98154$
> > $\Delta m = 0.2415\,u$
> >
> > **Step 2: Calculate Energy ($E_B$)**
> > Method 1 (using conversion factor):
> > $E_B = 0.2415 \times 931.5\,MeV$
> > $\enclose{box}{E_B \approx 225\,MeV}$
> >
> > Method 2 (using kg and Joules as per slide):
> > $\Delta m_{kg} = 0.2415 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 4.009 \times 10^{-28}\,kg$
> > $E = mc^2 = (4.009 \times 10^{-28})(3 \times 10^8)^2 = 3.608 \times 10^{-11}\,J$
> > Convert to MeV: $\frac{3.608 \times 10^{-11}}{1.60 \times 10^{-13}} \approx 225.5\,MeV$

> [!sq]- Example 12-3: Binding Energy for Iron
> Calculate the binding energy per nucleon for ${}_{26}^{56}Fe$. ($M_{Fe} = 55.93494\,u$).
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **Given:**
> > $Z = 26$, $N = 30$.
> >
> > **Step 1: Mass Defect**
> > $\Delta m = [26(1.007825) + 30(1.008665)] - 55.93494$
> > $\Delta m = [26.20345 + 30.25995] - 55.93494$
> > $\Delta m = 56.4634 - 55.93494 = 0.52846\,u$
> >
> > **Step 2: Total Binding Energy**
> > $E_B = 0.52846 \times 931.5 = 492.26\,MeV$
> > (Slide calculation yields $493.44\,MeV$ using slightly different constants).
> >
> > **Step 3: Binding Energy per Nucleon**
> > $\frac{E_B}{A} = \frac{493.44}{56}$
> > $\enclose{box}{\frac{E_B}{A} \approx 8.8\,MeV/\text{nucleon}}$

> [!formula]- Binding Energy per Nucleon
> A measure of stability.
> $\text{BE per nucleon} = \frac{E_B}{A}$
> > [!unit]- $E_B$
> > Total binding energy
> 
> > [!unit]- $A$
> > Mass number (total nucleons)

> [!liststart]- Stability Curve
> ![[Pasted image 20260203132605.png]]
> > [!continue]- Curve Characteristics
> > * The curve peaks around $A = 56$ (Iron), which is the ||most stable|| nucleus.
> > * For low $A$, stability increases with mass number (Fusion region).
> > * For high $A$, stability decreases slowly (Fission region).

> [!sq]- Example 12-4: Binding Energy of the last neutron
> What is the binding energy of the last neutron in ${}_{6}^{13}C$?
> Given mass ${}_{6}^{12}C = 12.0000\,u$ and ${}_{6}^{13}C = 13.003355\,u$.
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **Concept:**
> > Consider the reaction: ${}_{6}^{12}C + n \rightarrow {}_{6}^{13}C$.
> > The energy required to remove the neutron is the difference in mass energy.
> >
> > **Calculation:**
> > Mass of parts = $M(^{12}C) + m_n = 12.0000 + 1.008665 = 13.008665\,u$
> > Mass of whole = $13.003355\,u$
> > $\Delta m = 13.008665 - 13.003355 = 0.00531\,u$
> > $E = 0.00531 \times 931.5$
> > $\enclose{box}{E = 4.95\,MeV}$
## Nuclear Stability and Forces
> [!liststart]- Nuclear Stability ($N$ vs $Z$ Curve)
> ![[Pasted image 20260203132735.png]]
> > [!continue]- $N=Z$ Line
> > For light nuclei, stability occurs when $N \approx Z$.
> 
> > [!continue]- Deviation
> > As $Z$ increases, the number of $\color{orange}i$ must increase faster than the number of $\color{orange}ii$ to maintain stability (to overcome $\color{orange}iii$).
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > > neutrons
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > > protons
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iii$
> > > Coulomb repulsion

> [!continue]- Strong nuclear force is
> yhe force that binds nucleons together.
> * **Range:** Very ||short-range|| ($\approx 10^{-15}\,m$).
> * **Strength:** Much stronger than the electric force at short distances.
> * **Property:** It is essentially zero if nucleons are more than $10^{-15}\,m$ apart.

> [!continue]- Weak nuclear force is
> force that governs the decaying of nuclei that are unstable

> [!info]- Role of Neutrons
> More massive nuclei require extra neutrons to overcome the ||Coulomb repulsion|| of the protons in order to be stable.

# 12.3 Radioactivity, Alpha Decay, Beta Decay, and Gamma Decay

> [!timeline]- Historical context
> > [!continue]- Toward the end of the $19^{th}$ century, minerals were found that
> > would darken a photographic plate even in the absence of light.
> 
> > [!continue]- This phenomenon is now called
> > radioactivity
> 
> > [!name]- Marie and Pierre Curie
> > isolated two new elements that were highly radioactive; they are now called ||polonium|| and ||radium|| 

> [!definition]- Radioactivity
> The $\color{orange}i$ and $\color{orange}ii$ emission of radiation when an unstable nuclide decays.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > random
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > spontaneous

> [!liststart]- Characteristics of Radioactive Decay
> > [!continue]- Spontaneous
> > The reaction is unplanned and cannot be predicted. It is independent of ||physical conditions (e.g., pressure, temperature)|| and ||chemical changes||.
> 
> > [!continue]- Random
> > The probability of a nucleus decaying at a given instant is the ||same|| for all nuclei in the sample.

> [!table]- Comparison of Radioactive Radiations
> | Property | Alpha ($\alpha$) | Beta ($\beta$) | Gamma ($\gamma$) |
> | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
> | **Particle** | Helium Nucleus (${}_2^4He$) | Electron (${}_{-1}^0e$) or Positron (${}_{+1}^0e$) | Photon |
> | **Charge** | $+2e$ | $-1e$ or $+1e$ | Neutral ($0$) |
> | **Mass** | Heavy ($\approx 4u$) | Light ($\approx 1/1840 u$) | Zero |
> | **Penetration Power** | Weak (stopped by paper) | Moderate (stopped by 3mm Al) | Strong (stopped by cm of Lead) |
> | **Ionization Power** | Strong | Moderate | Weak |
> | **Deflection in E/B Fields** | Yes | Yes (Opposite to Alpha) | No |
> 
> All has ability to affect ||a photographic plate|| and produce ||fluorescence||

## Alpha Decay ($\alpha$)
> [!info]- Symbol
> $^{4}_{2}\ce{He}$ or $^{4}_{2}\,\alpha$

> [!continue]- Process
> An unstable nucleus emits an alpha particle (a helium nucleus).
> $$^{A}_{Z}N\to ^{A-4}_{Z-2}N'+^{4}_{2}\ce{He}$$
> * The parent nucleus loses $\color{orange}i$ protons and $\color{orange}ii$ neutrons.
> * The mass number $A$ decreases by $\color{orange}iii$ and atomic number $Z$ decreases by $\color{orange}iv$.
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > 2
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > 2
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iii$
> > 4
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iv$
> > 2

> [!example]- Example of $\alpha$ decay
> $^{226}_{88}\ce{Ra}\to ^{222}_{86}\ce{Rn}+^{4}_{2}\ce{He}$

> [!formula]- Alpha Decay Equation
> ${}_{Z}^{A}X \rightarrow {}_{Z-2}^{A-4}Y + {}_{2}^{4}He$
> > [!unit]- $X$
> > Parent Nucleus
> 
> > [!unit]- $Y$
> > Daughter Nucleus
> 
> > [!unit]- $He$
> > Alpha particle

> [!definition]- Disintegration energy
> The difference in mass between the parent nucleus with the sum of the daughter nucleus and the alpha particle

> [!formula]- Disintegration Energy, $Q$
> The energy released during decay (due to mass difference).
> $Q = (M_P - M_D - m_{\alpha})c^2$
> > [!unit]- $Q$
> > Disintegration energy in $J$ or $MeV$
> 
> > [!unit]- $M_P$
> > Mass of Parent nucleus
> 
> > [!unit]- $M_D$
> > Mass of Daughter nucleus
> 
> > [!unit]- $m_{\alpha}$
> > Mass of Alpha particle

> [!sq]- Example 12-5: Uranium decay energy
> Calculate the disintegration energy when ${}_{92}^{232}U$ ($232.037156\,u$) decays to ${}_{90}^{228}Th$ ($228.028741\,u$) with the emission of an $\alpha$ particle ($4.002603\,u$).
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **Reaction:**
> > ${}_{92}^{232}U \rightarrow {}_{90}^{228}Th + {}_{2}^{4}He$
> >
> > **Step 1: Calculate Mass Difference**
> > Mass of products = $228.028741 + 4.002603 = 232.031344\,u$
> > Mass defect $\Delta m = \text{Mass Parent} - \text{Mass Products}$
> > $\Delta m = 232.037156 - 232.031344 = 0.005812\,u$
> >
> > **Step 2: Calculate Energy**
> > $Q = 0.005812 \times 931.5\,MeV$
> > $\enclose{box}{Q = 5.41\,MeV}$

## Beta Decay ($\beta$)

> [!liststart]- Types of Beta Decay
> > [!continue]- Beta-Minus Decay
> > > [!info]- Symbol
> > > $^{0}_{-1}e$ or $\beta^{-}$
> > 
> > A neutron turns into a proton and an electron.
> > ${}_{Z}^{A}N \rightarrow {}_{Z+1}^{A}N' + {}_{-1}^{0}e + \bar{\nu}$
> > * **Emits:** An electron and an ||antineutrino ($\bar{\nu}$)||.
> > * **Change:** $Z$ increases by 1, $A$ remains constant.
> > 
> > > [!example]- $\beta$ minus decay
> > > $^{234}_{90}\ce{Th}\to ^{234}_{91}\ce{Pa}+^{0}_{-1}e+\nu$
> > > $^{234}_{91}\ce{Pa}\to ^{234}_{92}\ce{U}+^{0}_{-1}e+\nu$
> > > $^{214}_{83}\ce{Bi}\to ^{214}_{84}\ce{Po}+^{0}_{-1}e+\nu$
> 
> > [!continue]- Beta-Plus Decay
> > > [!info]- Symbol
> > > $^{0}_{1}e$ or $\beta^{+}$
> > 
> > A proton turns into a neutron and a positron.
> > $_{Z}^{A}N \rightarrow _{Z-1}^{A}N' + _{+1}^{0}e + \nu$
> > * **Emits:** A positron and a ||neutrino ($\nu$)||.
> > * **Change:** $Z$ decreases by 1, $A$ remains constant.

> [!continue]- The Neutrino
> * Neutrinos were proposed to conserve ||energy|| and ||momentum|| in beta decay.
> * They are uncharged and have negligible mass.
> 
> > [!info]- Symbol
> > $\nu$

> [!sq]- Example 12-6: Energy release in C-14 decay
> How much energy is released when ${}_{6}^{14}C$ ($14.003242\,u$) decays to ${}_{7}^{14}N$ ($14.003074\,u$) by $\beta^-$ emission?
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **Reaction:**
> > ${}_{6}^{14}C \rightarrow {}_{7}^{14}N + {}_{-1}^{0}e$
> > (Note: Atomic masses usually include electron masses, so for $\beta^-$ decay, the electron masses cancel out if using neutral atom masses).
> >
> > **Calculation:**
> > $\Delta m = 14.003242 - 14.003074 = 0.000168\,u$
> > $Q = 0.000168 \times 931.5$
> > $\enclose{box}{Q \approx 0.156\,MeV}$

> [!continue]- Beta decay can also occur where the nucleus emits
> a positron rather than an electron
> $^{19}_{10}\ce{Ne}\to ^{19}_{9}\ce{F}+e^{+}+\nu$
> > [!continue]- And a nucleus can
> > capture one of it's inner electrons
> > $^{7}_{4}\ce{Be}+e^{-}\to ^{7}_{3}\ce{Li}+\nu$
## Gamma Decay ($\gamma$)

> [!continue]- Process
> Emission of high-energy photons (gamma rays).
> * Occurs when a nucleus in an $\color{orange}i$ state makes a transition to a $\color{orange}ii$ state.
> * Often follows alpha or beta decay.
> * **No change** in $A$ or $Z$.
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > excited
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > lower (or ground)

> [!example]- Examples of $\gamma$ decay
> $^{218}_{84}\ce{Po}^{*}\to ^{214}_{82}\ce{Pb}+^{4}_{2}\ce{He}+\gamma$
> $^{234}_{91}\ce{Pa}^{*}\to ^{234}_{92}\ce{U}+^{0}_{-1}e+\gamma$
> $^{208}_{81}\ce{Ti}^{*}\to ^{208}_{81}\ce{Ti}+\gamma$

# 12.4 Half-Life and Rate of Decay

> [!continue]- Nature of Decay
> Nuclear decay is a $\color{orange}i$ process. The decay of any nucleus is not influenced by the decay of any other.
> * The number of parent nuclei in a sample is continually $\color{orange}ii$.
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > random
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > decreasing

> [!formula]- Rate of Decay (Activity)
> The number of decays in a short time interval is proportional to the number of nuclei present.
> $\frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N$
> $A = \lambda N$
> > [!unit]- $\frac{dN}{dt}$ (or $A$)
> > Activity (Decay rate) in decays per second ($s^{-1}$) or Becquerel ($Bq$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $\lambda$
> > Decay constant in $s^{-1}$
> 
> > [!unit]- $N$
> > Number of radioactive nuclei present at that instant
> 
> > [!info]- Note on Sign
> > The minus sign indicates that $N$ is ||decreasing||.

> [!formula]- Radioactive Decay Law (Exponential Decay)
> $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$
> $A = A_0 e^{-\lambda t}$
> > [!unit]- $N$
> > Number of nuclei remaining at time $t$
> 
> > [!unit]- $N_0$
> > Initial number of nuclei at $t=0$
> 
> > [!unit]- $\lambda$
> > Decay constant in $s^{-1}$
> 
> > [!unit]- $t$
> > Time elapsed in seconds ($s$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $A$
> > Activity at time $t$
> 
> > [!graph]- $N_{0}$ to $t$ and $\frac{dN}{dt}$ to $t$
> > ![[Pasted image 20260203200418.png]]

> [!definition]- Half-Life, $T_{1/2}$
> The time it takes for $\color{orange}i$ the nuclei in a given sample to decay.
> * It is related to the decay constant.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > half

> [!formula]- Half-Life Equation
> $T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} \approx \frac{0.693}{\lambda}$
> > [!unit]- $T_{1/2}$
> > Half-life in seconds ($s$) (or days, years, etc.)
> 
> > [!unit]- $\lambda$
> > Decay constant in $s^{-1}$ (or $days^{-1}$, $years^{-1}$)

> [!sq]- Example 7: Decay of Nuclide A
> Nuclide A decays into stable nuclide B. The half-life of A is $5.0$ days. Initial number $N_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{20}$. Calculate number of B after 20 days.
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **Given:**
> > $T_{1/2} = 5.0\,days$
> > $N_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{20}$
> > $t = 20\,days$
> >
> > **Step 1: Decay Constant**
> > $\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{T_{1/2}} = \frac{0.693}{5} = 0.139\,days^{-1}$
> >
> > **Step 2: Remaining Nuclei A ($N$)**
> > $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$
> > $N = (1.0 \times 10^{20}) e^{-(0.139)(20)}$
> > $N = 1.0 \times 10^{20} \times e^{-2.78}$
> > $N \approx 6.2 \times 10^{18}\,\text{nuclei}$
> >
> > **Step 3: Nuclei B Formed**
> > $N_B = N_0 - N$
> > $N_B = 1.0 \times 10^{20} - 6.2 \times 10^{18}$
> > $N_B = 100 \times 10^{18} - 6.2 \times 10^{18}$
> > $\enclose{box}{N_B = 9.38 \times 10^{19}\,\text{nuclei}}$

> [!sq]- Example 8: Randomness and Decay
> 80% of a radioactive substance decays in 4.0 days.
> > [!continue]- $a)$ Explain "Random" and "Spontaneous".
> > * **Random:** Time for decay of each nucleus cannot be ||predicted||; probability is constant.
> > * **Spontaneous:** Happens by itself without ||external stimuli|| (unaffected by temperature/pressure).
>
> > [!continue]- $b)$ Determine decay constant and half-life.
> > **Given:**
> > "80% decays" $\implies$ 20% remains.
> > $N = 0.20 N_0$ at $t = 4.0\,days$.
> >
> > **i. Decay Constant ($\lambda$):**
> > $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$
> > $0.20 N_0 = N_0 e^{-\lambda(4)}$
> > $\ln(0.20) = -4\lambda$
> > $-1.609 = -4\lambda$
> > $\lambda = \frac{1.609}{4}$
> > $\enclose{box}{\lambda \approx 0.402\,days^{-1}}$
> >
> > **ii. Half-Life ($T_{1/2}$):**
> > $T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} = \frac{0.693}{0.402}$
> > $\enclose{box}{T_{1/2} \approx 1.72\,days}$

> [!sq]- Example 9: Phosphorus-32 Activity
> P-32 ($ \lambda = 5.6 \times 10^{-7}\,s^{-1}$) emits $4.0 \times 10^7$ beta particles/sec.
> > [!continue]- $a)$ Half-life.
> > $T_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} = \frac{0.693}{5.6 \times 10^{-7}}$
> > $\enclose{box}{T_{1/2} \approx 1.24 \times 10^6\,s}$
>
> > [!continue]- $b)$ Mass of P-32.
> > **Find N:**
> > $A = \lambda N \implies 4.0 \times 10^7 = (5.6 \times 10^{-7})N$
> > $N = \frac{4.0 \times 10^7}{5.6 \times 10^{-7}} = 7.14 \times 10^{13}\,\text{nuclei}$
> >
> > **Find Mass:**
> > Molar Mass of P-32 $\approx 32\,g/mol$.
> > Mass $= \frac{N}{N_A} \times \text{Molar Mass}$
> > Mass $= \frac{7.14 \times 10^{13}}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} \times 32$
> > $\enclose{box}{\text{Mass} \approx 3.8 \times 10^{-9}\,g}$

> [!sq]- Example 10: Thorium-228
> Half-life = 1.913 years.
> > [!continue]- $a)$ Decay constant.
> > $t = 1.913 \times 365 \times 24 \times 3600 \approx 6.03 \times 10^7\,s$.
> > $\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{6.03 \times 10^7} \approx 1.15 \times 10^{-8}\,s^{-1}$
>
> > [!continue]- $b)$ Mass for 12.0 Ci activity.
> > **Convert Curie to Bq:**
> > $1\,Ci = 3.7 \times 10^{10}\,Bq$.
> > $A = 12.0 \times 3.7 \times 10^{10} = 4.44 \times 10^{11}\,Bq$.
> >
> > **Find N:**
> > $N = \frac{A}{\lambda} = \frac{4.44 \times 10^{11}}{1.15 \times 10^{-8}} = 3.86 \times 10^{19}\,\text{nuclei}$.
> >
> > **Find Mass (M = 228):**
> > Mass $= \frac{3.86 \times 10^{19}}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} \times 228$
> > $\enclose{box}{\text{Mass} \approx 1.46 \times 10^{-2}\,g}$

> [!sq]- Example 12-7: Sample Activity
> Sample contains $1.00 \times 10^{22}$ Carbon-14 nuclei ($T_{1/2} = 5730\,yr$).
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **Convert Half-life to seconds:**
> > $T_{1/2} = 5730 \times 3.156 \times 10^7\,s \approx 1.81 \times 10^{11}\,s$.
> > $\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{1.81 \times 10^{11}} \approx 3.83 \times 10^{-12}\,s^{-1}$.
> >
> > **Calculate Activity:**
> > $A = \lambda N = (3.83 \times 10^{-12})(1.00 \times 10^{22})$
> > $\enclose{box}{A \approx 3.83 \times 10^{10}\,\text{decays/s}}$

# 12.5 Decay Series

> [!continue]- Definition
> A decay series occurs when one radioactive isotope decays to another radioactive isotope, which decays to another, and so on.

> * **Parent:** The original nucleus (e.g., U-238).

# 12.6 Radioactive Dating

> [!continue]- Principle
> Using the known $\color{orange}i$ of isotopes to determine the age of materials.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > half-life

> [!liststart]- Common Methods
> > [!continue]- Uranium Dating
> > Uses U-238 ($T_{1/2} = 4.5 \times 10^9\,years$). Used to date the oldest rocks on Earth ($\approx 4$ billion years).
> 
> > [!continue]- Carbon Dating
> > Uses C-14 ($T_{1/2} = 5730\,years$). Used for dating organic materials.
