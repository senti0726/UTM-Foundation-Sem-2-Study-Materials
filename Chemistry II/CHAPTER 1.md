# 1.1 Fundamentals of Thermochemistry
## Introduction of Thermochemistry
> [!definition]- Thermochemistry
> Thermochemistry is a branch of thermodynamics that deals with heat involved and energy changes during a chemical reaction.

> [!definition]- Thermodynamics
> Thermodynamics is a detailed study of energy relations

> [!liststart]- Matter and energy undergoing changes
> All matter contains energy, whenever matter undergoes a change, the quantity of energy that the matter contains also changes
> > [!example]- burning candle melts a piece of ice - both chemical and physical changes
> > > [!continue]- chemical change
> > > higher energy reactants (wax and $\ce{O_{2}}$) form lower energy products ($\ce{CO_{2}}$ and $\ce{H_{2}O}$)
> > 
> > > [!continue]- physical changes
> > > heat is absorbed, ice turns to water

> [!example]- Gummy bear immolation
> > [!from]- Energy stored
> > Chemical energy is stored in the Gummy Bear, which is primarily sugar.
> 
> > [!to]- $\underrightarrow{\text{Energy Transfer}}$
> > If the Gummy Bear is placed in molten potassium chlorate, $\ce{KClO_{3}}$, the sugar is oxidised to $\ce{CO_{2}}$ and $\ce{H_{2}O}$
> 
> > [!to]- Energy released
> > the energy evolved in the chemical reaction is observed as heat and light

### **Units of Energy**
> [!definition]- Joule
> Joule is the amount of energy needed to move a $1\,kg$ mass by a distance $1\,m$
> > [!unit]- $1J=1N\cdot M=1kg\,m^{2}\,s ^{-2}$

> [!definition]- Calorie
> $1$ calorie is the energy needed to raise the temperature of $1$ gram of water by $1^{\circ}C$
> $1\,Cal=1\,kcal=1000\,cal$

> [!unit]- Convert to Joule:
> > [!continue]- $1\,cal$
> > $4.184\,J$
> 
> > [!continue]- $1\,Cal$
> > $4184\,J$
> 
> > [!continue]- $1\,kWh$
> > $3.60\times 10^{6}J$

## The First Law of Thermodynamics

> [!definition]- 
> Energy cannot be created or destroyed
> > [!info]- Also known as
> > Law of Conservation of Energy

> [!formula]- Mathematical Statement
> The energy of the universe is constant.
> $E_{universe} = E_{system} + E_{surroundings} = 0$
> 
> For a system, the change in internal energy ($\Delta E$) is:
> $\Delta E = q + w$
> > [!unit]- $E$
> > Energy in $J$
> 
> > [!unit]- $q$
> > Heat absorbed or released
> 
> > [!unit]- $w$
> > Work done ($w = P\Delta V$)
> 
> > [!table]- Sign Conventions for $q$ and $w$
> > 
> > | Variable | Positive (+) | Negative (-) |
> > | :--- | :--- | :--- |
> > | **Heat ($q$)** | System $\color{orange}i$ heat (Endothermic) | System $\color{orange}ii$ heat (Exothermic) |
> > | **Work ($w$)** | System $\color{orange}iii$ energy from work (Work done ON system) | System $\color{orange}iv$ energy by doing work (Work done BY system) |
> > | **Internal Energy ($\Delta E$)** | System $\color{orange}v$ internal energy | System $\color{orange}vi$ internal energy |
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > > gains
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > > releases
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iii$
> > > gains
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iv$
> > > releases
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}v$
> > > gains
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}vi$
> > > loses

# 1.2 Enthalpy ($H$) and Reaction Profiles

## Enthalpy at Constant Temperature

> [!definition]- Enthalpy ($H$)
> Refers to the heat content of a system.
> > [!info]- Common Units
> > Calories ($cal$ or $kcal$), Joule ($J$), and Newton meter ($Nm$).

> [!continue]- Enthalpy is similar to Internal Energy ($E$) but
> is defined for chemical reactions that occur at $\color{orange}i$.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > constant pressure

> [!formula]- Enthalpy Relationship
> We cannot measure absolute enthalpy, but we can measure enthalpy change ($\Delta H$).
> $\Delta H = \Delta U + P\Delta V$
> > [!unit]- $\Delta H$
> > Enthalpy change
> 
> > [!unit]- $\Delta U$
> > Change in internal energy (energy due to movement of matter)
> 
> > [!unit]- $P\Delta V$
> > Energy due to volume occupation by matter
> 
> > [!liststart]- Conditions
> > > [!continue]- At Constant Pressure ($\Delta P = 0$)
> > > $\Delta H = q_p$
> > 
> > > [!continue]- At Constant Volume ($\Delta V = 0$)
> > > $\Delta H = q_v$

> [!formula]- Enthalpy Change Definition
> $\Delta H = H_{final} - H_{initial}$
> > [!formula]- For Chemical Reaction
> > $\Delta H_{reaction} = H_{products} - H_{reactants}$
> 
> > [!continue]- $\Delta H_\text{rxn}$
> > > [!unit]- usually have the unit of 
> > > $kJ\,{mol_\text{rxn}}^{-1}$
> > 
> > > [!continue]- May also be called $\Delta H_\text{combustion}$ if it is a
> > > combustion reaction
> > > > [!tip]- These reactions are always exothermic
> > 
> > > [!continue]- May also be called $\Delta H_\text{neutralisation}$ if it is a
> > > acid-base neutralisation reaction

## Heat, Specific Heat, and Temperature

> [!formula]- Heat of Reaction ($q$)
> $q = m \times s \times \Delta T$
> > [!unit]- $q$
> > Heat of reaction in Joules ($J$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $m$
> > Mass of substance in grams ($g$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $s$
> > Specific heat capacity in $J\,^\circ C^{-1}\,g^{-1}$
> > > [!formula]- Relation to Heat Capacity ($C$)
> > > $s = \frac{C}{m}$ or $C = m \times s$
> 
> > [!unit]- $\Delta T$
> > Temperature difference ($T_{final} - T_{initial}$) in $^\circ C$

## Exothermic vs. Endothermic Processes

> [!compare]- Exothermic vs Endothermic
> > [!continue]- Exothermic Process
> > Involves the $\color{orange}i$ of energy ($q$) from the system into the surroundings.
> > * **System:** Temperature $\color{orange}ii$.
> > * **Surroundings:** Temperature $\color{orange}iii$.
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > > release
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > > decreases
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iii$
> > > increases ($T_f > T_i$)
> 
> > [!continue]- Endothermic Process
> > Involves the $\color{orange}iv$ of energy from the surroundings into the system.
> > * **System:** Temperature $\color{orange}v$.
> > * **Surroundings:** Temperature $\color{orange}vi$.
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iv$
> > > absorption
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}v$
> > > increases
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}vi$
> > > decreases ($T_f < T_i$)

> [!table]- Sign Conventions for Enthalpy Changes
> | Quantity | Exothermic | Endothermic |
> | :--- | :--- | :--- |
> | **Heat ($q_{surr}$)** | $\color{orange}i$ | $\color{orange}ii$ |
> | **Temp Change ($\Delta T$)** | $\color{orange}iii$ ($T_f > T_i$) | $\color{orange}iv$ ($T_f < T_i$) |
> | **Enthalpy Change ($\Delta H$)** | $\color{orange}v$ ($\Delta H < 0$) | $\color{orange}vi$ ($\Delta H > 0$) |
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > Positive ($+ve$)
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > Negative ($-ve$)
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iii$
> > Positive ($+ve$)
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iv$
> > Negative ($-ve$)
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}v$
> > Negative ($-ve$)
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}vi$
> > Positive ($+ve$)

> [!definition]- Thermochemical Equation
> An equation that contains $\Delta H$ as part of the equation with the states of species indicated.
> > [!tip]- Must specify states
> > Always indicate $(s)$, $(l)$, $(g)$, or $(aq)$.

> [!sq]- Example: Exothermic Equation
> $H_2(g) + 1/2 O_2(g) \rightarrow H_2O(l)$
> > [!continue]- Interpretation
> > $\Delta H = -q\,kJ$
> > Heat is a **product** of the reaction.
> > $H_2 + 1/2 O_2 \rightarrow H_2O + q\,kJ$
> 
> > [!continue]- Reverse Reaction
> > $H_2O(l) \rightarrow H_2(g) + 1/2 O_2(g)$
> > $\Delta H = +q\,kJ$ (Becomes Endothermic)

> [!sq]- Example: Endothermic Equation
> $H_2O(l) \rightarrow H_2(g) + 1/2 O_2(g)$
> > [!continue]- Interpretation
> > $\Delta H = +q\,kJ$
> > Heat is a **reactant** (or used).
> > $q\,kJ + H_2O \rightarrow H_2 + 1/2 O_2$

## Calculations for Solution Calorimetry

> [!formula]- Heat of Reaction Equation
> Heat of reaction = (Heat absorbed by surroundings) + (Heat absorbed by calorimeter)
> $q = (m \times s \times \Delta T) + (C_p \times \Delta T)$
> > [!unit]- $m$
> > Total mass of solution in grams ($g$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $s$
> > Specific heat capacity of the solution ($J\,g^{-1}\,^\circ C^{-1}$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $\Delta T$
> > Temperature change ($T_{final} - T_{initial}$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $C_p$
> > Heat capacity of the calorimeter ($J\,^\circ C^{-1}$)

> [!liststart]- The big 3 capacities
> > [!definition]- Specific heat capacity of a substance
> > $\color{teal}\text{the amount of heat required to raise the temperature}$ of $\color{red} 1g \text{ of the substance}$ $\color{teal}\text{by } 1^{\circ}C$
> 
> > [!definition]- Heat capacity ($C_{p}$) of a calorimeter
> > $\color{teal}\text{the amount of heat required to raise the temperature}$ of $\color{red}\text{a calorimeter and its content}$ $\color{teal}\text{by } 1^{\circ}C$
> 
> > [!definition]- molar heat capacity
> > $\color{teal}\text{the heat to raise the temperature}$ of $\color{red} 1\,mol \text{ of substance}$ $\color{teal}\text{by } 1^{\circ}C$
> > 
> > > [!info]- Simplification
> > > Most calorimeters absorb negligible heat, therefore $C_p$ may be ignored.
> > > $q \approx m \times s \times \Delta T$

> [!formula]- Enthalpy of Reaction from Calorimetry
> $\Delta H = -\frac{q}{n}$
> > [!unit]- $q$
> > Heat absorbed/released by the reaction ($J$ or $kJ$)
> 
> > [!unit]- $n$
> > Moles of the ||limiting reactant||

> [!formula]- Determining $n$ from Volume and Molarity
> $n=\frac{MV}{1000}$
> where
> > [!unit]- $n$
> > number of moles in $mol$
> 
> > [!unit]- $M$
> > Molarity in $M$
> 
> > [!unit]- $V$
> > Volume in $mL$
> > > [!tip]- if its $L$, the formula becomes
> > > $n=MV$

> [!sq]- Sample Problem 3: Calibration & Measurement
> **Part 1 (Calibration):** $1.22\,g$ Benzoic Acid ($M_r=122$, $\Delta H_c = -3230\,kJ/mol$) causes $\Delta T = 0.82^\circ C$.
> **Part 2 (Measurement):** $0.9\,g$ Glucose ($M_r=180$) causes $\Delta T = 0.36^\circ C$.
> Calculate $\Delta H_c$ for Glucose.
> > [!continue]- Step 1: Find Calorimeter Constant ($C$)
> > Moles Benzoic Acid $n = 1.22 / 122 = 0.01\,mol$.
> > Heat released $q = n \times \Delta H_c = 0.01 \times 3230 = 32.3\,kJ$.
> > $C = \frac{q}{\Delta T} = \frac{32.3}{0.82} = 39.39\,kJ\,^\circ C^{-1}$.
> 
> > [!continue]- Step 2: Calculate $\Delta H$ for Glucose
> > Heat released by Glucose $q_{gluc} = C \times \Delta T_{gluc}$
> > $q_{gluc} = 39.39 \times 0.36 = 14.18\,kJ$.
> > Moles Glucose $n = 0.9 / 180 = 0.005\,mol$.
> > $\Delta H = -\frac{14.18}{0.005}$
> > $\enclose{box}{\Delta H = -2836\,kJ/mol}$

# 1.4 Standard Enthalpy Changes

## Standard Conditions & States

> [!continue]- Need for Standardization
> To compare enthalpy changes fairly, chemists established a reference point called the **standard state**.

> [!definition]- Enthalpy change
> the amount of heat evolved or absorbed in a reaction carried out at a constant pressure
> > [!info]- Symbol

### **1. Standard Enthalpy of Formation**

> [!definition]- Standard Enthalpy of Formation
> The enthalpy change when one mole of compound is formed from its element in standard states
> > [!info]- Symbol
> > $\Delta H^{o}_{f}$

> [!info]- Abbreviation
> $\Delta H^{o}_{f}[compound(standard\,state\,g,s\,or\,l)]$

> [!example]- Standard Enthalpy of formation of water
> $\ce{H_{2}(g) +\frac{1}{2}O_{2}(g)->H_{2}O(l)}\;\Delta H^{o}_{f}=-286\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> > [!continue]- What this equation is trying to tell you is that:
> > > [!definition]- The standard enthalpy of formation of water is defined as
> > > the heat released when $1\,mol$ of liquid water is formed from $\ce{H_{2}}$ gas at $1\,atm$ and $25^{\circ}C$
> > 
> > > [!continue]- It's value is 
> > > $-286kJ$
> > 
> > > [!continue]- Abbreviated as
> > > $\Delta H^{o}_{f}[\ce{H_{2}O}(l)]=-286\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$

> [!example]- Standard Enthalpy of formation of carbon dioxide gas
> $\ce{C(s,graphite) +O_{2}(g)->CO_{2}(g)}\;\Delta H^{o}_{f}=-393kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> > [!continue]- What this equation is trying to tell you is that
> > > [!definition]- The standard enthalpy of formation of carbon dioxide is defined as
> > > The heat released when $1\,mol$ of $\ce{CO_{2}}$ gas is formed from $\ce{C}$ in solid (graphite) and $\ce{O_{2}}$ gas at $1\,atm$ and $25^{\circ}C$
> > 
> > > [!continue]- It's value is
> > > $-393kJ$
> > 
> > > [!continue]- Abbreviated as
> > > $\ce{\Delta H^{o}_{f}[CO_{2}(g)]}=-393kJ\,mol^{-1}$

> [!liststart]- Key Rules
> > [!continue]- Coefficient
> > The product must always have a coefficient of ||1||. (Reactants can have fractions).
> 
> > [!continue]- Stability
> > The standard enthalpy of formation for any pure element in its most stable form is ||zero||.
> 
> > [!continue]- Stability of Compounds
> > Compounds with large negative $\Delta H_f^\circ$ are energetically ||more stable|| than their elements.

> [!sq]- Example: $\ce{SiH_{4}}$
> State the definition and write the corresponding thermochemical equation based on the following abbreviation.$$\Delta H^{o}_{f}[\ce{SiH_{4}(g)}]=+33\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$$
> > [!continue]- $i)$ Definition
> > The standard enthalpy of formation of Silicon Tetrahydride, $\ce{SiH_{4}}$ is defined as the $\overbrace{\text{heat gained}}^{\Delta H>0}$ when $1\,mol$ of Silicon Tetrahydride is formed from silicon in solid and Hydrogen in gas at $1\,atm$ and $25^{\circ}C$
> 
> > [!continue]- $ii)$ Thermochemical Equation
> > $\ce{Si(g) +2H_{2}(g)->SiH_{4}(g)}\;\Delta H=+33kJ\,mol^{-1}$

> [!sq]- Example: Determining Pattern of $H^{o}_{f}$
> The following are not standard enthalpy formation. Why?
> > [!continue]- $a)$ $\ce{2C(s) +2O_{2}(g)->2CO_{2}(g)}$
> > The product is not in $1\,mole$ 
> 
> > [!continue]- $b)$ $\ce{2CO(g) +2O_{2}(g)->2CO_{2}(g)}$
> >  - The reactants can only contain elements, not compound
> >  - The product is not in $1\,mole$
### **2. Standard Enthalpy of Combustion**


> [!formula]- General Equation
> 1 mole Compound + $O_2$ $\rightarrow$ Products
> > [!example]- Methane Combustion
> > $CH_4(g) + 2O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g) + 2H_2O(l)$

> [!continue]- Abbreviation
> $\Delta H^{o}_{c}['\text{compound}','\text{standard state}']$

> [!info]- Sign Convention
> Combustion is always ||exothermic|| ($\Delta H$ is negative).

> [!example]-

> > 
> > > [!continue]- liquid
> > > $\ce{C_{5}}-\ce{C_{17}}$
> > 
> > > [!continue]- solid
> > > $\ce{C_{>18}}$

> $\ce{C(s,graphite) +O_{2}(g)->CO_{2}(g)}\;\;\Delta H^{o}_{c}=-393kJ\,mol^{-1}$

> > > [!continue]- The amount of heat released is

### **3. Standard Enthalpy of Neutralization**

> [!definition]- Definition
> The enthalpy change when **one mole of water** ($H_2O$) is formed from the reaction of an acid ($H^+$) and a base ($OH^-$) under standard conditions ($1.0\,M$).
> > [!info]- Symbol
> > $\Delta H_n^\circ$

> [!formula]- Ionic Equation
> $H^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) \rightarrow H_2O(l)$
> $\Delta H_n^\circ \approx -57.3\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$ (for strong acid/base)

> [!unit]- In standard conditions, the concentration of acid and base (alkali) is
> $1.0mol\,dm^{-3}$

> [!compare]- Strong vs Weak Systems
> > [!continue]- Strong Acid + Strong Base
> > Dissociate ||completely||.
> > Value is almost constant at ||$-57.3\,kJ$||.
> 
> > [!continue]- Weak Acid or Weak Base
> > Dissociate ||partially||.
> > Value is ||less negative|| (e.g., $-50.4\,kJ$).
> > * **Reason:** Some energy is absorbed to ||dissociate the weak acid/base||.

> [!definition]- Polyprotic acid 
> Specific acids that are capable of losing more than a single proton per molecule in acid-base reactions.
> $\textcolor[RGB]{100,100,100}{\text{acids that have more than one ionizable }\ce{H+}\text{ atom per molecule}}$

> [!formula]- The $\Delta H^{o}_{n}$ of a polyprotic acid is the average $\Delta H_{n}$'s of the neutralisation steps
> > [!example]- $\ce{H_{2}SO_{4}}$
> > $\ce{2NaOH +H_{2}SO_{4}-> Na_{2}SO_{4} +2H_{2}O}\;\;\Delta H_{n}=\frac{-61.9+(-70.90))}{2}=-66.4kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> > $1^{st}\;\ce{H+}$: $\ce{NaOH +H_{2}SO_{4}->NaHSO_{4} +H_{2}O}$
> > $@\Delta H=-61.95kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> > $2^{nd}\:\ce{H+}$: $\ce{NaOH +NaHSO_{4}->Na_{2}SO_{4} +H_{2}O}$
> > $\Delta H=-70.90kJ$

### **5. Standard Enthalpy of Solution**

> [!definition]- Definition
> Heat change when **1 mole of a solute** is dissolved in a large excess of water to form a dilute solution.
> > [!info]- Symbol
> > $\Delta H_{soln}^\circ$

> [!formula]- General equation
> $\ce{MX(s) +water->M+(aq) +X-(aq)}$

> [!liststart]- Two-Stage Process
> The dissolution process involves two steps:
> > [!continue]- 1. Lattice Breaking
> > Breaking the crystal lattice into gaseous ions.
> > Requires energy equal to **-Lattice Energy** ($-\Delta H_{latt}$).
> > $\ce{MX(s)->M+(g) +X-(g)}\;\;-\Delta H_\text{latt}$
> 
> > [!continue]- 2. Hydration
> > Gaseous ions become hydrated (surrounded by water).
> > Releases **Hydration Energy** ($\Delta H_{hyd}$).
> > $\ce{M+(g) +water->M+(aq)}\;\;\Delta H_\text{HydCation}$
> > $\ce{X-(g) +water->X-(aq)}\;\;\Delta H_\text{HydAnion}$

> [!formula]- Formula
> $\Delta H_{soln} = -\Delta H_{latt} + \Delta H_{hyd(cation)} + \Delta H_{hyd(anion)}$

> [!definition]- Lattice Energy ($\Delta H_{latt}$)
> Enthalpy change when 1 mole of crystalline solid is formed from **gaseous ions**.
> * Process is ||exothermic|| (formation) or ||endothermic|| (breaking).

> [!definition]- Hydration Energy ($\Delta H_{hyd}$)
> Heat released when 1 mole of **gaseous ions** is hydrated in water.
> * Process is always ||exothermic|| (negative).

> [!sq]- Exercise 6: Calculating $\Delta H_{soln}$
> Calculate enthalpy of solution for NaCl.
> Given: $\Delta H_{hyd}(Na^+) = -390\,kJ$, $\Delta H_{hyd}(Cl^-) = -380\,kJ$, $\Delta H_{latt}(NaCl) = -776\,kJ$.
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > $\Delta H_{soln} = -(-776) + (-390) + (-380)$
> > $\Delta H_{soln} = 776 - 770$
> > $\enclose{box}{\Delta H_{soln} = +6\,kJ\,mol^{-1}}$

# 1.5 Hess's Law

> [!timeline]- Historical Context
> **Germain Henri Hess** (1802–1850) was a Swiss-Russian chemist and doctor who formulated Hess's Law, an early principle of thermochemistry.

## Principle of Hess's Law

> [!definition]- Hess's Law
> States that the heat involved (enthalpy change) in a chemical reaction is **independent** of the $\color{orange}i$ by which the chemical change occurs.
> * If a reaction occurs in two or more steps, the enthalpy for the reaction is the $\color{orange}ii$ of the enthalpies of the individual steps.
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > routes (or path)
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > sum

> [!liststart]- Energy Cycle Diagram Concept
> Consider the reaction $A + B \rightarrow C + D$.
> ```tikz
> \begin{document}
> \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.5, thick]
>   % --- Define Energy Levels ---
>   \coordinate (Reactants) at (0, 4);
>   \coordinate (Intermediate) at (1.5, 2);
>   \coordinate (Products) at (0, 0);
>   \def\lineW{4} % Width of the main lines
> 
>   % --- Draw Horizontal Energy Lines ---
>   % Reactant Line (Top)
>   \draw (Reactants) -- ++(\lineW, 0) node[right] {Reactant A + B};
>   
>   % Intermediate Line (Middle - shorter)
>   \draw (Intermediate) -- ++(2, 0);
>   
>   % Product Line (Bottom)
>   \draw (Products) -- ++(\lineW, 0) node[right] {Product C + D};
> 
>   % --- Draw Arrows and Labels ---
>   % Direct Path (Delta H1)
>   \draw[->, dashed, >=stealth] (0.8, 4) -- (0.8, 0);
>   \node[left] at (0.8, 2) {$\Delta H_1$};
> 
>   % Indirect Path - Step 1 (Delta H2)
>   \draw[->, dashed, >=stealth] (2.5, 4) -- (2.5, 2);
>   \node[right] at (2.5, 3) {$\Delta H_2$};
> 
>   % Indirect Path - Step 2 (Delta H3)
>   \draw[->, dashed, >=stealth] (2.5, 2) -- (2.5, 0);
>   \node[right] at (2.5, 1) {$\Delta H_3$};
> 
> \end{tikzpicture}
> \end{document}
> ```
> > [!continue]- Path 1 (Direct)
> > Direct conversion with enthalpy change $\Delta H_1$.
> 
> > [!continue]- Path 2 (Indirect)
> > Conversion via an intermediate step with enthalpies $\Delta H_2$ and $\Delta H_3$.
> 
> > [!formula]- Relationship
> > Since the total energy change is path-independent:
> > $\Delta H_1 = \Delta H_2 + \Delta H_3$

> [!sq]- Example: Formation of $CO_2$
> Carbon can burn to form $CO_2$ directly, or form $CO$ first and then $CO_2$.
> ```tikz
> \begin{document}
> \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.5, thick, >=stealth]
> % --- Y-axis ---
> \draw[->] (-0.5, 0) -- (-0.5, 4.5) node[above] {H};
> 
> % --- Energy Levels ---
> % Top Level (Reactants)
> \draw (0, 4) -- (6, 4);
> \node[above] at (1.5, 4) {C(s) + O$_2$(g)};
> 
> % Middle Level (Intermediate)
> \draw (3, 2.8) -- (6, 2.8);
> \node[above] at (4.5, 2.8) {CO(g) + $\frac{1}{2}$O$_2$(g)};
> 
> % Bottom Level (Product)
> \draw (0, 0) -- (6, 0);
> \node[above] at (1.5, 0) {CO$_2$(g)};

## Calculation Methods

### **Method 1: Hess's Law Equation**

> [!formula]- The Summation Formula
> Used when standard enthalpies of formation ($\Delta H_f^\circ$) are known for all reactants and products.
> $\Delta H_{reaction} = \sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{Products}) - \sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{Reactants})$

> [!sq]- Exercise 7: Reaction Enthalpy Calculation
> Calculate the standard enthalpy of reaction for:
> $C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}(s) + 12O_2(g) \rightarrow 12CO_2(g) + 11H_2O(l)$
> (Note: Source text has a typo "C12H12O6" but molecular weight suggests sucrose-like or similar. Let's strictly follow the numbers in Slide 72).
> **Reaction from Slide 72:** $C_{12}H_{12}O_{6}(s) + 6O_2(g) \rightarrow 6CO_2(g) + 6H_2O(l)$
> **Given Data:**
> $\Delta H_f^\circ[C_{12}H_{12}O_6] = -1258\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> $\Delta H_f^\circ[H_2O(l)] = -286\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> $\Delta H_f^\circ[CO_2(g)] = -392\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> 
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **Step 1: Sum of Products**
> > $\sum \Delta H_f(Prod) = [6 \times \Delta H_f(CO_2)] + [6 \times \Delta H_f(H_2O)]$
> > $= [6(-392)] + [6(-286)]$
> > $= -2352 - 1716 = -4068\,kJ$
> > 
> > **Step 2: Sum of Reactants**
> > $\sum \Delta H_f(React) = [1 \times \Delta H_f(C_{12}H_{12}O_6)] + [6 \times \Delta H_f(O_2)]$
> > $= [-1258] + [6(0)]$ (Element in standard state is 0)
> > $= -1258\,kJ$
> > 
> > **Step 3: Total Enthalpy**
> > $\Delta H_{rxn} = (\text{Sum Products}) - (\text{Sum Reactants})$
> > $\Delta H_{rxn} = -4068 - (-1258)$
> > $\enclose{box}{\Delta H_{rxn} = -2810\,kJ\,mol^{-1}}$

### **Method 2: Algebraic Summation of Equations**

> [!procedure]- Procedure
> 1.  Write the **Target Equation** (the reaction you want to find $\Delta H$ for).
> 2.  Manipulate known thermochemical equations to match the target:
>       * **Reverse** equations: Flip the sign of $\Delta H$.
>       * **Multiply** coefficients: Multiply $\Delta H$ by the same factor.
> 3.  **Add** the manipulated equations together. Cancel out species that appear on both sides.

> [!sq]- Exercise 8: Combustion of Methane
> Calculate the enthalpy of combustion of methane ($\Delta H_c$).
> **Given Data:**
> (1) $\Delta H_f(CH_4) = -74\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> (2) $\Delta H_f(CO_2) = -393\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> (3) $\Delta H_f(H_2O) = -286\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> > [!continue]- Step 1: Write Target Equation
> > Combustion of Methane ($CH_4$):
> > $CH_4(g) + 2O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g) + 2H_2O(l)$
> 
> > [!continue]- Step 2: Write Formation Equations
> > (1) $C(s) + 2H_2(g) \rightarrow CH_4(g)$ ($\Delta H = -74$)
> > (2) $C(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g)$ ($\Delta H = -393$)
> > (3) $H_2(g) + \frac{1}{2}O_2(g) \rightarrow H_2O(l)$ ($\Delta H = -286$)
> 
> > [!continue]- Step 3: Manipulate Equations
> > * **Reverse Eq (1)** to put $CH_4$ on the reactant side:
> >     $CH_4(g) \rightarrow C(s) + 2H_2(g)$
> >     $\Delta H = +74\,kJ$
> > * **Keep Eq (2)** as is (to get $1 CO_2$ on product side):
> >     $C(s) + O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g)$
> >     $\Delta H = -393\,kJ$
> > * **Multiply Eq (3) by 2** (to get $2 H_2O$ on product side):
> >     $2H_2(g) + O_2(g) \rightarrow 2H_2O(l)$
> >     $\Delta H = 2(-286) = -572\,kJ$
> 
> > [!continue]- Step 4: Add and Cancel
> > Reactants: $CH_4 + \cancel{C} + \cancel{2H_2} + O_2 + O_2$
> > Products: $\cancel{C} + \cancel{2H_2} + CO_2 + 2H_2O$
> > Net: $CH_4(g) + 2O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g) + 2H_2O(l)$
> >
> > **Total Enthalpy:**
> > $\Delta H_c = (+74) + (-393) + (-572)$
> > $\enclose{box}{\Delta H_c = -891\,kJ\,mol^{-1}}$

# 1.6 Lattice Energy & The Born-Haber Cycle

> [!timeline]- Context
> The **Born-Haber Cycle** is an approach to analyze reaction energies. It was developed in 1919 by German scientists **Max Born** and **Fritz Haber**.

> [!definition]- Born-Haber Cycle
> Energy cycle for ionic compounds
## Lattice Energy ($\Delta H_{lattice}$)

> [!definition]- Lattice Energy
> The enthalpy change when **one mole of crystalline solid** is formed from **gaseous ions** under standard conditions.
> * **Process:** $M^+(g) + X^-(g) \rightarrow MX(s)$
> * **Sign:** Always ||exothermic|| (negative) because bonds are formed.

> [!info]- Indirect Measurement
> Lattice energy cannot be directly measured from experiments. It is indirectly calculated using the ||Born-Haber Cycle||.

## Related Energy Definitions

> [!definition]- Ionization Energy ($\Delta H_{ion}$)
> The minimum energy needed to remove an electron from **1 mole of free gaseous atoms** to form gaseous cations.
> * **Process:** $M(g) \rightarrow M^+(g) + e^-$
> * **Sign:** Always ||endothermic|| (positive).

> [!definition]- Electron Affinity ($\Delta H_{affinity}$)
> The energy involved when **1 mole of electrons** is gained by **1 mole of neutral gaseous atoms** to form gaseous anions.
> * **Process:** $X(g) + e^- \rightarrow X^-(g)$
> * **Sign:** Usually ||exothermic|| (negative) for the first electron affinity.

## Constructing the Born-Haber Cycle

> [!formula]- Hess's Law Application
> The sum of energies in the clockwise path equals the direct formation energy.
> $\Delta H_f^\circ = \Delta H_{at}(Na) + \Delta H_{at}(Cl) + \Delta H_{ion} + \Delta H_{affinity} + \Delta H_{lattice}$

> [!sq]- Exercise 9: Calculating Lattice Energy
> Calculate the lattice energy for the formation of NaCl(s).
> **Given Data:**
> $\Delta H_f(NaCl) = -411\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> $\Delta H_{at}(Na) = +108\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> $\Delta H_{ion}(Na) = +500\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> $\Delta H_{at}(Cl) = +121\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> $\Delta H_{affinity}(Cl) = -364\,kJ\,mol^{-1}$
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > **Formula:**
> > $\Delta H_{lattice} = \Delta H_f - [\Delta H_{at}(Na) + \Delta H_{at}(Cl) + \Delta H_{ion} + \Delta H_{affinity}]$

> > **Calculation:**
> > Sum of intermediate steps = $108 + 121 + 500 + (-364)$
> > Sum = $729 - 364 = 365\,kJ$
> >
> > $\Delta H_{lattice} = -411 - 365$
> > $\enclose{box}{\Delta H_{lattice} = -776\,kJ\,mol^{-1}}$
