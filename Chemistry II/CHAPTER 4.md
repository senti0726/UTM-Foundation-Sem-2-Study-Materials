### 1. Arrhenius Theory (1884)


> [!definition]- Arrhenius Acid
> A substance that generates $\color{orange}i$ in aqueous solutions.
> * Example: $\ce{HCl(aq) -> H+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > hydrogen ions ($\ce{H+}$)

> [!definition]- Arrhenius Base
> A substance that generates $\color{orange}ii$ in aqueous solutions.
> * Example: $\ce{NaOH(aq) -> Na+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > hydroxide ions ($\ce{OH-}$)

> [!liststart]- Classification of Arrhenius Acids
> > [!continue]- Monoprotic acid
> > Generates **one** $\ce{H+}$ ion per molecule.
> > * e.g., $\ce{HCl}$
>
> > [!continue]- Diprotic acid
> > Generates **two** $\ce{H+}$ ions per molecule.
> > * e.g., $\ce{H2SO4(aq) -> 2H+(aq) + SO4^{2-}(aq)}$
>
> > [!continue]- Triprotic acid
> > Generates **three** $\ce{H+}$ ions per molecule.
> > * e.g., $\ce{H3PO4(aq) -> 3H+(aq) + PO4^{3-}(aq)}$

 > [!disadvantage]- Disadvantages
> > [!continue]- Arrhenius theory for acids and bases are limited to
> > compounds containing $\ce{H}$ and $\ce{OH}$ respectively.
> 
> > [!continue]- Only applicable to
> > aqueous solutions.
> 
> > [!continue]- Does not explain how
> > > [!continue]- molecular substances
> > > > [!continue]- such as $\ce{NH_{3}}$
> > > > dissolve in water to form basic solutions,
> > > > > [!continue]- even though
> > > > > they do not contain $\ce{OH^{-}}$ ions.
> > > 
> > > > [!continue]- such as $\ce{CO_{2}}$, dissolve in water to form acidic solutions
> > > > > [!continue]- even though
> > > > > they do not contain $\ce{H^{+}}$ ions.
> > 
> > > [!continue]- ionic compounds
> > > such as $\ce{Na_{2}CO_{3}}$ or $\ce{Na_{2}O}$, dissolve in water to form basic solutions,
> > > > [!continue]- even though
> > > > they do not contains $\ce{OH^{-}}$/


### 2. Brønsted-Lowry Theory (1923)


> [!definition]- Brønsted-Lowry Acid
> A substance that **donates** a $\color{orange}i$ to another substance.
> * It is a proton ||donor||.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > proton ($\ce{H+}$)
> 
> > [!tip]- Like:
> > 100 plus, being plus (positive), and full of acid, throws out regenerative potions that cause your energy and health go +++(donating proton)

> [!definition]- Brønsted-Lowry Base
> A substance that **accepts** a $\color{orange}ii$ from another substance.
> * It is a proton ||acceptor||.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > proton

> [!compare]- Brønsted-Lowry Theory
> > [!advantage]- Brønsted-Lowry Theory
> > allows reaction to be reversible
> > $\ce{H -A +}\overbrace{\ce{:B}}^{\text{accept}\ce{\,H+}\,\text{proton}}\ce{\Leftrightarrow :A- +H -B+}$
> > > [!continue]- The original base has
> > > an extra $\ce{H+}$ after the reaction, so it will act as an $\color{orange}i$ in the reverse process
> > > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > > > acid
> > 
> > > [!continue]- The original acid has
> > > a lone pair of electrons after the reaction, so it will act as a $\color{orange}i$ in the reverse process
> > > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > > > base
> 
> > [!disadvantage]- Brønsted-Lowry Theory
> > > [!continue]- Limited to compounds with
> > > ionisable $\ce{H}$ atoms only.
> > 
> > > [!example]- 
> > > $\ce{NaOH}$ is an Arrhenius base but it is not a Brønsted-Lowry base because it does not accept a proton.
> > > $\ce{NaOH(s) +H+ -> \text{no reaction}}$

> [!compare]- Conjugate Acid-Base Pairs
> A pair which differs by the gain or loss of a proton ($\ce{H+}$).
> > [!continue]- Conjugate Base
> > The species formed when a proton is $\color{orange}i$ from an acid.
> > * An acid becomes its conjugate base when it $\color{orange}ii$ a proton.
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > > removed
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > > donates
>
> > [!continue]- Conjugate Acid
> > The species formed when a proton is $\color{orange}i$ to a base.
> > * A base becomes its conjugate acid when it $\color{orange}ii$ a proton.
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > > added
> > 
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > > accepts
> 
> > [!example]- Example of Bronsted-Lowry acid in water solvent
> > Water acts as Bronsted-Lowry base
> > $\overbrace{\ce{HA}}^{\text{Acid}} +\underbrace{\ce{H_{2}O}}_{\text{Base}}\Leftrightarrow\overbrace{\ce{A^{-}}}^{\text{Conjugate base}} +\underbrace{\ce{H_{3}O^{+}}}_{\text{Conjugate acid}}$
> 
> > [!tip]- Quite literally:
> > Oh you're an acid that just gave up what made you an acid? You're conj base now.

> [!sq]- Example 1: Ammonia
> $\ce{NH3(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$
> > [!continue]- Analysis
> > > [!continue]- **Forward:** 
> > > $\ce{NH3}$ accepts a proton to become $\ce{NH4+}$.
> > > > [!continue]- Base: 
> > > > $\ce{NH3}$
> > > 
> > > > [!continue]- Conjugate Acid: 
> > > > $\ce{NH4+}$
> > 
> > > [!continue]- **Reverse:** 
> > > $\ce{NH4+}$ donates a proton to become $\ce{NH3}$.

> [!sq]- Example 2: Carbonate
> $\ce{CO3^{2-}(aq) + H2O(l) <=> HCO3^{-}(aq) + OH-(aq)}$
> > [!continue]- Analysis
> > * $\ce{CO3^{2-}}$ accepts a proton $\rightarrow$ **Base**.
> > * $\ce{H2O}$ donates a proton $\rightarrow$ **Acid**.

> [!definition]- Amphoteric Substance
> A substance that can act as either an **acid** or a **base**.
> > [!because]- Reason
> > They have both a transferable $\ce{H}$ and an atom with lone pair electrons.
> > > [!example]- Example: **Water** ($\ce{H2O}$).
> > > > [!continue]- Acts as base 
> > > > $\ce{HCl + H2O -> Cl- + H3O+}$ (Accepts $\ce{H+}$)
> > > 
> > > > [!continue]- Acts as acid 
> > > > $\ce{NH3 + H2O <=> NH4+ + OH-}$ (Donates $\ce{H+}$)
> > > 
> > > > [!continue]- Therefore
> > > > water is amphoteric

### 3. Lewis Theory (1938)

> [!definition]- Lewis Acid
> A substance that **accepts** an $\color{orange}i$ to form a covalent bond.
> * Electron deficient, therefore it is an ||electrophile||.
> * Examples: $\ce{BF3}, \ce{CO2}, \ce{SO2}, \ce{Al^3+}, \ce{H+}$.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > electron pair
> 

> [!definition]- Lewis Base
> A substance that **donates** an $\color{orange}ii$ to form a covalent bond.
> * Electron rich, therefore it is a ||nucleophile||.
> * Examples: $\ce{NH3}, \ce{OH-}, \ce{CN-}, \ce{H2O}$.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > electron pair
> 
> > [!tip]- 
> > Baseball player deadass launches two balls (electron pair)

> [!example]- Electron transfer between boron trifluoride and ammonia
> ![[CHAPTER 4 2026-02-06 16.05.10.excalidraw]]
> > [!equal]- $\ce{BF_{3}}\equiv$
> > Lewis acid (accepts an electron pair to form a covalent bond between $\ce{B}$ and $\ce{N}$)
> 
> > [!equal]- $\ce{NH_{3}}\equiv$
> > Lewis base (donates an electron pair to form a bond)

> [!example]- $\ce{B[OH]4^{-}}$
> ![[CHAPTER 4 2026-02-06 16.15.39.excalidraw]]
> > [!equal]- $\ce{B(OH)3}\equiv$
> > Lewis acid (accepts an electron pair to form a covalent bond between $\ce{B}$ and $\ce{O}$)
> 
> > [!equal]- $\ce{OH-}\equiv$
> > Lewis base (donates an electron pair to form a bond)

> [!example]- Examples of Lewis Acids
> > [!continue]- A metal cation
> > e.g. $\ce{Al^3+}$, $\ce{Cr^3+}$
>
> > [!continue]- Central atom incomplete octet
> > Boron in $\ce{BF3}$
>
> > [!continue]- A molecule with delocalised ($\pi$) electrons
> > involving two atoms with different electronegativities involving oxygen.
> > * e.g. $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{SO2}$, $\ce{SO3}$

> [!example]- Examples of Lewis base
> > [!continue]- Negatively charged ions
> > $\ce{CN-}$, $\ce{OH-}$
> 
> > [!continue]- Central atom has lone pair electrons
> > oxygen in $\ce{H_{2}O}$, $\ce{NH_{3}}$
> 
> > [!continue]- ||Anions|| are better Lewis Bases than ||neutral atoms or molecules||
> > $\ce{N:\,<\,N:^{-}}$
> 
> > [!continue]- Generally, the more ||electronegative|| an atom, the ||less willing|| it is to be a Lewis Base
> > $\ce{O:\,<\,S:}$

> [!advantage]- The Lewis theory 
> allows most substances without $\ce{H+}$ to be defined as acids

> [!sq]- Exercise: Identify Lewis Acid/Base
> Identify the Lewis acids and Lewis bases in the following reactions:
> (a) $\ce{AlCl3 + Cl- -> AlCl4-}$
> (b) $\ce{Co^{3+} + 6NH3 -> [Co(NH3)6]^{3+}}$
> > [!continue]- Solution (a)
> > * $\ce{AlCl3}$: Accepts electron pair from $\ce{Cl-}$. **Lewis Acid**.
> > * $\ce{Cl-}$: Donates electron pair. **Lewis Base**.
>
> > [!continue]- Solution (b)
> > * $\ce{Co^{3+}}$: Metal cation accepts electrons. **Lewis Acid**.
> > * $\ce{NH3}$: N has lone pair to donate. **Lewis Base**.

# 4.2 The Ion Product Constant of Water ($K_w$) and pH

> [!formula]- Autoionization of Water
> $\ce{2H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$
> or simplified: $\ce{H2O(l) <=> H+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$
>
> **Ion Product Constant ($K_w$):**
> $K_w = [\ce{H+}][\ce{OH-}]$
> > [!info]- Value
> > At $25^{\circ}C$ (room temp), $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$.
> 
> > [!unit]- $K_{w}$
> > the ion product constant of water
> 
> > [!unit]- $\ce{[H+]}$
> > molarity of $\ce{H+}$ in solution
> 
> > [!unit]- $\ce{OH-}$
> > molarity of $\ce{OH-}$ in solution

> [!formula]- pH and pOH
> > [!continue]- **pH:** 
> > $pH = -\log[\ce{H+}]$
> 
> > [!continue]- **pOH:** 
> > $pOH = -\log[\ce{OH-}]$
> 
> > [!continue]- **Relationship:** 
> > $pH + pOH = 14$

> [!liststart]- Classification of Solutions (at $25^{\circ}C$)
> > [!continue]- Neutral
> > $[\ce{H+}] = [\ce{OH-}] = 1.0 \times 10^{-7}\,M$.
> > $pH = 7.0$.
> > > [!example]- 
> > > - Blood
> > > - Pure water
> > > - Milk
>
> > [!continue]- Acidic
> > $[\ce{H+}] > [\ce{OH-}]$.
> > $pH < 7.0$.
> > > [!example]- 
> > >  - Orange juice
> > >  - Pickles
>
> > [!continue]- Basic
> > $[\ce{H+}] < [\ce{OH-}]$.
> > $pH > 7.0$.
> > > [!example]- 
> > >  - Ammonia
> > >  - Milk of Magnesia

> [!sq]- Sample Problem 1
> The concentration of $\ce{OH-}$ ions in a certain household ammonia cleaning solution is $0.0025\,M$. Calculate the concentration of $\ce{H+}$.
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > Given $[\ce{OH-}] = 0.0025\,M$.
> > Assume room temperature ($25^{\circ}C$), $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$.
> > $K_w = [\ce{H+}][\ce{OH-}]$
> > $1.0 \times 10^{-14} = [\ce{H+}](0.0025)$
> > $[\ce{H+}] = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{0.0025}$
> > $\enclose{box}{[\ce{H+}] = 4.0 \times 10^{-12}\,M}$

> [!sq]- Sample Problem 2
> Calculate the concentration of $\ce{OH-}$ ions in a $\ce{HCl}$ solution whose hydrogen ion concentration is $1.3\,M$.
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > Given $[\ce{H+}] = 1.3\,M$.
> > $K_w = [\ce{H+}][\ce{OH-}]$
> > $1.0 \times 10^{-14} = (1.3)[\ce{OH-}]$
> > $[\ce{OH-}] = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{1.3}$
> > $\enclose{box}{[\ce{OH-}] = 7.7 \times 10^{-15}\,M}$

# 4.3 The Strengths of Acids and Bases

## Factors Affecting Acid Strength

> [!continue]- Strength of acids and bases may be compared in terms of their
> > [!continue]- Ionisation constants 
> > $K_{a}$ for acids and $K_{b}$ for bases
> 
> > [!continue]- Percent or degree of ionisation
> > $=\frac{\text{moles ionised per litre}}{\text{moles available per litre}}\times 100\%$

> [!info]- General Principles
> The strength of an acid depends on:
> > [!continue]- Ease of proton loss
> > How easily the proton ($\ce{H+}$) is lost or removed from an $\ce{H-X}$ bond.
> > * Depends on ||bond strength||.
>
> > [!continue]- Bond Polarity
> > The polarity between $\ce{H}$ and the atom to which it is bonded.

> [!mnemonic]- Types of Acids
> $\textcolor[RGB]{255,0,255}{\text{Bi}}$ased $\textcolor[RGB]{255,0,255}{\text{Pol}}$iticians $\textcolor[RGB]{255,0,255}{\text{Ox}}$idize $\textcolor[RGB]{255,0,255}{\text{Org}}$anisms
> > [!mnemonic-points]- $\mathbf{\textcolor[RGB]{255,255,0}{\text{Bi}}}$ased
> > **${\textcolor[RGB]{255,0,255}{\textbf{Bi}}}$nary Acids**
> > $\ce{H-X}$
> > > [!tip]- Acids consisting of two elements: Hydrogen and a non-metal (e.g., $\ce{HCl}, \ce{HBr}$).
>
> > [!mnemonic-points]- $\mathbf{\textcolor[RGB]{255,255,0}{\text{Pol}}}$iticians
> > **${\textcolor[RGB]{255,0,255}{\textbf{Pol}}}$yprotic Acids**
> > $\ce{H_{m}YO_{n}}$
> > > 	[!tip]- Acids capable of donating more than one proton/hydrogen atom (e.g., $\ce{H_{2}SO_{4}}, \ce{H_{3}PO_{4}}$).
>
> > [!mnemonic-points]- $\mathbf{\textcolor[RGB]{255,255,0}{\text{Ox}}}$idize
> > **${\textcolor[RGB]{255,0,255}{\textbf{Ox}}}$yacids**
> > $\ce{HYO_{n}}$
> > > [!tip]- Acids that contain Hydrogen, Oxygen, and a third element (e.g., $\ce{HNO_{3}}, \ce{HClO_{4}}$).
> > 
> > > [!to]- Also known as
> > > Oxoacid
>
> > [!mnemonic-points]- $\mathbf{\textcolor[RGB]{255,255,0}{\text{Org}}}$anisms
> > **${\textcolor[RGB]{255,0,255}{\textbf{Org}}}$anic Acids**
> > $\ce{R-COOH}$
> > > [!tip]- Carbon-based acids, usually containing a carboxyl group (e.g., $\ce{CH_{3}COOH}$).
### 1. Binary Acids ($\ce{H-X}$)
> [!liststart]- Trends in Periodic Table
> > [!continue]- Going down a Group (e.g., $\ce{HF} \to \ce{HI}$)
> > Acidity **increases**.
> > > [!continue]- Reason: Bond Strength
> > > * The size of atom X ||increases||.
> > > * The $\ce{H-X}$ bond gets ||longer and weaker||.
> > > * **Result:** $\ce{H+}$ is ||easier|| to remove.
> > > * Order: $\ce{HF} \ll \ce{HCl} < \ce{HBr} < \ce{HI}$.
> 
> > [!table]- Relative acid strength to Bond energy
> > | Relative acid strength       | $\ce{H-F}$ | $\ce{H-Cl}$ | $\ce{H-Br}$ | $\ce{HI}$ |
> > | ---------------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | ----------- | --------- |
> > | Bond energy ($kJ\,mol^{-1}$) | 570        | 432         |       366      |     298      |
>
> > [!continue]- Going across a Period (e.g., $\ce{H2O} \to \ce{HF}$)
> > Acidity **increases**.
> > > [!continue]- Reason: Electronegativity
> > > * The electronegativity of X ||increases||.
> > > * The $\ce{H-X}$ bond polarity ||increases||.
> > > * **Result:** $\ce{H+}$ is ||easier|| to remove.
> 
> > [!tip]- Direction of increasing acidity in the PTOE
> > $\searrow$
### 2. Oxoacids ($\ce{H-O-Y}$)
> [!liststart]- Factors affecting Oxoacids
> > [!continue]- Electronegativity of Central Atom (Y)
> > For acids with the same number of oxygens (e.g., $\ce{HClO}$ vs $\ce{HBrO}$).
> > * Acidity **increases** as electronegativity of Y ||increases||.
> > > [!continue]- Reason
> > > * More electronegative Y withdraws electron density from the $\ce{O-H}$ bond.
> > > * Bond becomes more ||polar|| and weaker.
> > > > [!example]- $\ce{HClO}>\ce{HBrO}$
> > > > ![[Pasted image 20260206165306.png]]
>
> > [!continue]- Number of Oxygen Atoms
> > For acids with the same central atom (e.g., $\ce{HClO}$ vs $\ce{HClO4}$).
> > * Acidity **increases** as the number of lone oxygens ||increases||.
> > > [!continue]- Reason
> > > * Lone oxygens pull electron density from the central atom.
> > > * Increases central atom's ability to pull electrons from the $\ce{O-H}$ bond.
> > > Example: $\ce{HClO} < \ce{HClO2} < \ce{HClO3} < \ce{HClO4}$ (Strongest).

### 4. Carboxylic Acids ($\ce{R-COOH}$)

> > [!continue]- Electron Donating Groups (Alkyl groups)

## Strong vs Weak Acids/Bases

> [!compare]- Strong vs Weak
> > [!continue]- Strong Acids/Bases
> > Dissociate **completely** in water.
> > * $\ce{HA(aq) + H2O(l) -> H3O+(aq) + A-(aq)}$

## Dissociation Constants ($K_a$ & $K_b$)

> [!definition]- Acid Dissociation Constant ($K_a$)
> A measure of the strength of a weak acid.
> > [!formula]- Consider the weak acid dissociation
> > $\ce{HA + H2O <=> H3O+ + A-}$
> > > [!unit]- $K_{a}$
> > > Acid dissociation constant
> > > $K_a = \frac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}$
> > > > [!continue]- can be expressed in
> > > > $-\log$ value where
> > > > $\text{p}K_{a}=-\log K_{a}$
> 
> > [!continue]- Trends
> > * The **larger** the $K_a$, the ||stronger|| the acid.
> > * The **smaller** the $pK_a$ ($-\log K_a$), the ||stronger|| the acid.

> [!definition]- Base Dissociation Constant ($K_b$)
> A measure of the strength of a weak base.
> > [!formula]- 
> > $\ce{B + H2O <=> BH+ + OH-}$
> > > [!unit]- $K_b$
> > > Base dissociation constant
> > > $K_{b}= \frac{[\ce{BH+}][\ce{OH-}]}{[\ce{B}]}$
> > > > [!continue]- can also be expressed as
> > > > $\text{p}K_{b}=-\log K_{b}$
> 
> > [!continue]- Trends
> > * The **larger** the $K_b$, the ||stronger|| the base.
> > * The smaller the $\text{p}K_{b}$, the ||stronger|| the base

> [!formula]- Relationship between $K_a$ and $K_b$
> For a conjugate acid-base pair:
> $K_a \times K_b = K_w$
> > [!continue]- Derivation
> > $\begin{array}{l} & \cancel{ \ce{HA(aq)} }  & + & \ce{H_{2}O(l) } & \rightleftharpoons & \ce{H_{3}O+(aq)}  & +& \cancel{ \ce{A-(aq)} } & K_{a}K_{b} \\ & \cancel{ \ce{A-(aq)} } & + & \ce{H_{2}O(l)}& \rightleftharpoons & \cancel{\ce{HA(aq)} } & + & \ce{OH-(aq)}& K_{w}=\ce{[H_{3}O+][OH-]} \\\hline  &\ce{H_{2}O(l)} & + & \ce{H_{2}O(l)}&\rightleftharpoons & \ce{H_{3}O+(aq)} & +& \ce{OH-}\end{array}$
> > $K_{a}\times K_{b}=\frac{\ce{[A-][H_{3}O+]}}{\ce{[HA]}}\times \frac{\ce{[HA][OH-]}}{\ce{A-}}$
> > $K_{a}+K_{b}=\ce{[H_{3}O+][OH-]}=K_{w}$
> > $\enclose{box}{\text{At }25^{\circ}C,\,K_{w}=1\times 10^{-14}}$
> 
> > [!formula]- Logarithmic Form
> > $pK_a + pK_b = pK_w = 14$ (at $25^{\circ}C$)
> > > [!continue]- Derivation
> > > $(K_{a}\times K_{b}=K_{w})-\log$
> > > $-\log K_{a}+-\log K_{b}=-\log K_{w}$
> > > $pK_{a}+pK_{b}=pK_{w}$
> > > $pK_{a}+pK_{b}=-\log1\times 10^{-14}$
> > > $pK_{a}+pK_{b}=14$


## Calculations

> [!sq]- Sample Problem: pH of Strong Acid/Base
> (a) Calculate pH of $0.0750\,M$ solution of $\ce{HI}$.
> (b) Calculate pH of $0.055\,M$ solution of $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$.
> > [!continue]- Solution (a)
> > $\ce{HI}$ is a strong acid (dissociates completely).
> > $[\ce{H+}] = [\ce{HI}] = 0.075\,M$.
> > $pH = -\log(0.075) = \enclose{box}{1.12}$
>
> > [!continue]- Solution (b)
> > $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ is a strong base.
> > $\ce{Ca(OH)2 -> Ca^{2+} + 2OH-}$.
> > $[\ce{OH-}] = 2 \times 0.055 = 0.11\,M$.
> > $pOH = -\log(0.11) = 0.96$.
> > $pH = 14 - 0.96 = \enclose{box}{13.04}$

> [!sq]- Sample Problem: pH of Weak Acid
> Given $K_a = 6.8 \times 10^{-4}$.
> > [!continue]- $(a)\,i.$Calculate the pH of a $0.100\,M$ solution of $\ce{HF}$.
> > Given $K_a = 6.8 \times 10^{-4}$.
> > > [!continue]- Step 1: ICE Table
> > >
> > > | Species | $\ce{HF}$ | $\ce{H+}$ | $\ce{F-}$ |
> > > | :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
> > > | **I**nitial | $0.100$ | $0$ | $0$ |
> > > | **C**hange | $-x$ | $+x$ | $+x$ |
> > > | **E**quilibrium | $0.100-x$ | $x$ | $x$ |
> >
> > > [!continue]- Step 2: $K_a$ Expression
> > > $K_a = \frac{[\ce{H+}][\ce{F-}]}{[\ce{HF}]} = 6.8 \times 10^{-4}$
> > > $\frac{x^2}{0.100 - x} = 6.8 \times 10^{-4}$
> > > *Assumption:* Since $K_a$ is small, assume $x \ll 0.100$, so $0.100 - x \approx 0.100$.
> > > $\frac{x^2}{0.1} = 6.8 \times 10^{-4} \implies x^2 = 6.8 \times 10^{-5}$.
> > > $x = \sqrt{6.8 \times 10^{-5}} = 8.25 \times 10^{-3}\,M$.
> >
> > > [!continue]- Step 3: Calculate pH
> > > $[\ce{H+}] = x = 8.25 \times 10^{-3}\,M$.
> > > $pH = -\log(8.25 \times 10^{-3}) = \enclose{box}{2.08}$
> 
> > [!continue]- $(a)ii.$ The percent ionisation of $\ce{HF}$
> > $\text{Percent ionisation}=\frac{mol\text{ ionised per }\ell}{mol\text{ available per } \ell}\times 100\%$
> > $=\frac{8.25\times 10^{-3}mol}{0.1mol}\times100\%$
> > $\enclose{box}{=8.25\%}$
> 

> [!formula]- Percent Ionization
> $\% \text{ Ionization} = \frac{\text{conc. ionized}}{\text{initial conc.}} \times 100\%$

> [!sq]- Sample Problem: pH of Weak Base
> What is the pH of a $0.025\,M$ solution of hydrazine, $\ce{N2H4}$?

> > > [!failure]- $\ce{N_{2}H_{4}(aq)->N+(aq) +\cancel{ \ce{H+} }}$

> > > [!success]- $\ce{N_{2}H_{4}(aq) +H_{2}O(l)->N_{2}H_{5}+(aq) +OH-(aq)}$
> 
> > [!continue]- Step 1: ICE Table
> > $\ce{N2H4 + H2O <=> N2H5+ + OH-}$
> >
> > | Species | $\ce{N2H4}$ | $\ce{N2H5+}$ | $\ce{OH-}$ |
> > | :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
> > | **E**quilibrium | $0.025-x$ | $x$ | $x$ |

> > [!continue]- Step 2: Solve for x
> > $K_b = \frac{x^2}{0.025} = 1.7 \times 10^{-6}$.

# 4.4 Salts and Hydrolysis

## Definitions

> [!definition]- Salt
> an ionic compound formed from the reaction of an **acid** and a **base**.

> [!definition]- Salt Hydrolysis
> The reaction of an anion or cation of a salt with **water**. 
> * It usually affects the $\color{orange}i$ of the solution.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > pH

> [!formula]- Percent Hydrolysis
> $\% \text{ Hydrolysis} = \frac{[\text{Ions}]_{\text{Hydrolysed}}}{[\text{Ions}]_{\text{Initial}}} \times 100$

## Types of Salts and pH

> [!liststart]- Classification based on Parent Acid/Base
> > [!continue]- Neutral Salt
> > > [!continue]- Derived from
> > > Strong Acid + Strong Base.
> >
> > > [!continue]- Example
> > > $\ce{NaCl}$ (from $\ce{HCl} + \ce{NaOH}$). 
> >
> > > [!continue]- pH
> > > Neutral ($pH = 7$).
>
> > [!continue]- Acidic Salt
> > > [!continue]- Derived from
> > > Strong Acid + Weak Base.
> >
> > > [!continue]- Example
> > > $\ce{NH4Cl}$ (from $\ce{HCl} + \ce{NH3}$). 
> >
> > > [!continue]- pH
> > > Acidic ($pH < 7$).
>
> > [!continue]- Basic Salt
> > > [!continue]- Derived from
> > > Weak Acid + Strong Base. 
> >
> > > [!continue]- Example
> > > $\ce{CH3COONa}$ (from $\ce{CH3COOH} + \ce{NaOH}$).
> >
> > > [!continue]- pH
> > > Basic ($pH > 7$).

> > > [!continue]- Derived from

> >
> > > [!continue]- Example

> >
> > > [!continue]- pH

## Detailed Hydrolysis Mechanisms

### 1. Neutral Salt

> [!liststart]- Mechanism ($\ce{NaCl}$)
> > [!continue]- Dissociation
> > $\ce{NaCl(s) -> Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$. 
>
> > [!continue]- Ions Analysis
> > > [!continue]- Cation ($\ce{Na+}$)
> > > * Conjugate acid of a strong base ($\ce{NaOH}$).
> > > * It is a very ||weak|| conjugate acid.
> > > * Does **not** hydrolyze.
> >
> > > [!continue]- Anion ($\ce{Cl-}$)
> > > * Conjugate base of a strong acid ($\ce{HCl}$).
> > > * It is a very ||weak|| conjugate base.
> > > * Does **not** hydrolyze.
>
> > [!continue]- Result
> > No reaction with water. Solution remains **neutral**.

### 2. Acidic Salt

> [!liststart]- Mechanism ($\ce{NH4Cl}$)
> > [!continue]- Dissociation
> > $\ce{NH4Cl(s) -> NH4+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$. 
>
> > [!continue]- Ions Analysis
> > > [!continue]- Anion ($\ce{Cl-}$)
> > > * Conjugate base of strong acid.
> > > * Does ||not|| hydrolyze.
> >
> > > [!continue]- Cation ($\ce{NH4+}$)
> > > * Conjugate acid of a weak base ($\ce{NH3}$).
> > > * It is a ||strong|| conjugate acid.
> > > * **Undergoes hydrolysis.**
>
> > [!continue]- Hydrolysis Equation
> > $\ce{NH4+(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH3(aq) + H3O+(aq)}$
> > * Produces $\ce{H3O+}$, making the solution ||acidic||.

### 3. Basic Salt

> [!liststart]- Mechanism ($\ce{CH3COONa}$)
> > [!continue]- Dissociation
> > $\ce{CH3COONa(s) -> CH3COO-(aq) + Na+(aq)}$.
>
> > [!continue]- Ions Analysis
> > > [!continue]- Cation ($\ce{Na+}$)
> > > * Conjugate acid of strong base.
> > > * Does ||not|| hydrolyze. 
> >
> > > [!continue]- Anion ($\ce{CH3COO-}$)
> > > * Conjugate base of a weak acid ($\ce{CH3COOH}$).
> > > * It is a ||strong|| conjugate base.
> > > * **Undergoes hydrolysis.**
>
> > [!continue]- Hydrolysis Equation
> > $\ce{CH3COO-(aq) + H2O(l) <=> CH3COOH(aq) + OH-(aq)}$
> > * Produces $\ce{OH-}$, making the solution ||basic||.

### 4. Salt of Weak Acid & Weak Base

> [!liststart]- Mechanism ($\ce{NH4CN}$)
> > [!continue]- Analysis
> > Both ions are strong conjugates derived from weak parents.
> > > [!continue]- Cation ($\ce{NH4+}$)
> > > Hydrolyzes to form acidic $\ce{H3O+}$. 
> >
> > > [!continue]- Anion ($\ce{CN-}$)
> > > Hydrolyzes to form basic $\ce{OH-}$.
>
> > [!continue]- Result
> > Both ions hydrolyze. The pH depends on the **relative strength** ($K_a$ vs $K_b$) of the ions. 

## Determining pH of Salts

> [!sq]- Sample Problem (a): Acidic Salt
> Calculate the pH of a $0.1\,M$ solution of $\ce{NH4Cl}$.
> Given: $K_b$ of $\ce{NH4OH} (\ce{NH3}) = 1.75 \times 10^{-5}$.
> > [!continue]- Step 1: Salt Dissociation
> > $\ce{NH4Cl -> NH4+ + Cl-}$
> > $[\ce{NH4+}] = 0.1\,M$.
>
> > [!continue]- Step 2: Hydrolysis Equation (ICE Table)
> > $\ce{NH4+(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH3(aq) + H3O+(aq)}$
> >
> > | | $\ce{NH4+}$ | $\ce{NH3}$ | $\ce{H3O+}$ |
> > | :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
> > | **I** | $0.1$ | $0$ | $0$ |
> > | **C** | $-x$ | $+x$ | $+x$ |
> > | **E** | $0.1-x$ | $x$ | $x$ |
> > 
>
> > [!continue]- Step 3: Find $K_a$ (Hydrolysis Constant $K_h$)
> > Since $\ce{NH4+}$ acts as an acid, we need $K_a$.
> > $K_a = \frac{K_w}{K_b} = \frac{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}{1.75 \times 10^{-5}} = 5.7 \times 10^{-10}$.
>
> > [!continue]- Step 4: Solve for $x$
> > $K_a = \frac{[\ce{NH3}][\ce{H3O+}]}{[\ce{NH4+}]}$
> > $5.7 \times 10^{-10} = \frac{x^2}{0.1 - x} \approx \frac{x^2}{0.1}$ (Assume $x$ is small).
> > $x^2 = 5.7 \times 10^{-11}$.
> > $x = \sqrt{5.7 \times 10^{-11}} = 7.5 \times 10^{-6}\,M$.
> > $[\ce{H3O+}] = 7.5 \times 10^{-6}\,M$. 
>
> > [!continue]- Step 5: Calculate pH
> > $pH = -\log(7.5 \times 10^{-6}) = \enclose{box}{5.12}$.

> [!sq]- Sample Problem (b): Basic Salt
> Given the $K_h$ ($K_b$ for hydrolysis) of $\ce{CH3COONa}$ is $5.6 \times 10^{-10}$, calculate the pH of a $0.05\,M$ solution of the salt.
> > [!continue]- Step 1: Salt Dissociation
> > $\ce{CH3COONa -> CH3COO- + Na+}$
> > $[\ce{CH3COO-}] = 0.05\,M$.
>
> > [!continue]- Step 2: Hydrolysis Equation (ICE Table)
> > $\ce{CH3COO- + H2O <=> CH3COOH + OH-}$
> >
> > | | $\ce{CH3COO-}$ | $\ce{CH3COOH}$ | $\ce{OH-}$ |
> > | :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
> > | **I** | $0.05$ | $0$ | $0$ |
> > | **C** | $-x$ | $+x$ | $+x$ |
> > | **E** | $0.05-x$ | $x$ | $x$ |
> > 
>
> > [!continue]- Step 3: Solve for $x$
> > $K_h = \frac{[\ce{CH3COOH}][\ce{OH-}]}{[\ce{CH3COO-}]} = 5.6 \times 10^{-10}$.
> > $\frac{x^2}{0.05} = 5.6 \times 10^{-10}$.
> > $x^2 = 2.8 \times 10^{-11}$.
> > $x = \sqrt{2.8 \times 10^{-11}} = 5.29 \times 10^{-6}\,M$.
> > $[\ce{OH-}] = 5.29 \times 10^{-6}\,M$.
>
> > [!continue]- Step 4: Calculate pH
> > $pOH = -\log(5.29 \times 10^{-6}) = 5.28$.
> > $pH = 14 - 5.28 = \enclose{box}{8.72}$.

## Mechanism of Buffer Action

> [!continue]- How buffers work
> $\ce{H_{2}O(l) + HA(aq)\rightleftharpoons A-(aq) +H_{3}O+(aq)}$
> > [!continue]- Buffers work by applying
> > Le Châtelier’s Principle to weak acid equilibrium.
> 
> > [!continue]- Acidic buffer solutions contain
> > significant amounts of the weak acid molecules, $\ce{HA}$.
> > > [!continue]- These molecules ||neutralises|| the added base $\ce{OH-(aq)}$
> > > $\ce{HA(aq) +OH-(aq)->A-(aq) +H_{2}O(l)}$
> 
> > [!continue]- The buffer solution contains
> > significant amount of conjugate base anion, $\ce{A-}$.
> 
> > [!continue]- These ions
> > combine with added acid $\ce{H_{3}O+(aq)}$ to produce more $\ce{HA}$.
> > $\ce{A-(aq) +H_{3}O+(aq)->HA(aq) +H_{2}O(l)}$

> [!process]- How Acidic Buffers Work
> **System:** $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) <=> CH3COO-(aq) + H3O+(aq)}$
> Contains high concentrations of $\ce{CH3COOH}$ (reservoir to neutralize base) and $\ce{CH3COO-}$ (reservoir to neutralize acid).
>
> > [!continue]- If Strong Base ($\ce{OH-}$) is added
> > The added $\ce{OH-}$ reacts with the **weak acid** component.
> > * **Reaction:** $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + OH-(aq) -> CH3COO-(aq) + H2O(l)}$
> > * **Effect:**
> >     * $[\ce{CH3COOH}]$ ||decreases|| slightly.
> >     * $[\ce{CH3COO-}]$ ||increases|| slightly.
> >     * $\ce{H+}$ is ||reduced||.
> >     * Equilibrium shifts to the ||right||.
> >     * **Result:** pH remains almost constant. 
>
> > [!continue]- If Strong Acid ($\ce{H+}$) is added
> > The added $\ce{H+}$ reacts with the **conjugate base** component.
> > * **Reaction:** $\ce{CH3COO-(aq) + H+(aq) -> CH3COOH(aq)}$
> > * **Effect:**
> >     * $[\ce{CH3COO-}]$ ||decreases|| slightly.
> >     * $[\ce{CH3COOH}]$ ||increases|| slightly.
> >     * $\ce{OH-}$ is ||reduced||.
> >     * Equilibrium shifts to the ||left||.
> >     * **Result:** pH remains almost constant. 

> [!process]- How Basic Buffers Work
> **System:** $\ce{NH3(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$
> Contains high concentrations of $\ce{NH3}$ and $\ce{NH4+}$.
>
> > [!continue]- If Strong Acid ($\ce{H+}$) is added
> > The added $\ce{H+}$ reacts with the **weak base**.
> > * **Reaction:** $\ce{NH3 + H+ -> NH4+}$
> > * **Effect:**
> >     * $[\ce{NH3}]$ ||decreases||.
> >     * $[\ce{NH4+}]$ ||increases||.
> >     * $\ce{H+}$ is ||reduced||.
> >     * **Result:** $\ce{H+}$ is absorbed. 
>
> > [!continue]- If Strong Base ($\ce{OH-}$) is added
> > The added $\ce{OH-}$ reacts with the **conjugate acid**.
> > * **Reaction:** $\ce{NH4+ + OH- -> NH3 + H2O}$
> > * **Effect:**
> >     * $[\ce{NH4+}]$ ||decreases||.
> >     * $[\ce{NH3}]$ ||increases||.
> >     * $\ce{OH-}$ is ||reduced||.
> >     * **Result:** $\ce{OH-}$ is absorbed.

> [!info]- Summary of Changes
> When a buffer resists pH change:
> * Adding Acid $\to$ \[Weak Acid/Conj Acid\] ||increases||.
> * Adding Base $\to$ \[Weak Base/Conj Base\] ||increases||.
> * The ratio of components changes slightly, but the pH change is minimal compared to unbuffered water.

# 4.5 Buffer Solutions (Calculations)

## The Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation

> [!tip]- One of the two methods to determining the pH of a buffer solution, with the other being 
> Equilibrium Approach: solve using ICE table

> [!formula]- Derivation
> Starting from the dissociation of a weak acid:
> $\ce{HA(aq) + H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + A-(aq)}$
>
> $K_a = \frac{[\ce{H3O+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}$
>
> Rearranging for $[\ce{H3O+}]$:
> $[\ce{H3O+}] = K_a \times \frac{[\ce{HA}]}{[\ce{A-}]}$
>
> Taking the negative log ($-\log$) of both sides:
> $\enclose{box}{pH = pK_a + \log \frac{[\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}}$ 

> [!formula]- General Equation
> This equation allows us to calculate the pH of a buffer solution from the **initial concentrations** of the components.
>
> $pH = pK_a + \log \frac{[\text{base species}]}{[\text{acid species}]}$
> > [!info]- Conditions
> > Only valid when the "x is small" approximation holds (i.e., dissociation is minimal).

> [!liststart]- Specific Forms
> > [!continue]- For Acidic Buffer
> > $pH = pK_a + \log \left( \frac{[\text{conjugate base}]}{[\text{acid}]} \right)$
>
> > [!continue]- For Basic Buffer
> > Two approaches:
> > 1.  **Using pOH:**
> >     $pOH = pK_b + \log \left( \frac{[\text{conjugate acid}]}{[\text{base}]} \right)$
> >     $pH = 14 - pOH$
> > 2.  **Using pH directly (convert $pK_b$ to $pK_a$):**
> >     $pH = (14 - pK_b) + \log \left( \frac{[\text{base}]}{[\text{conjugate acid}]} \right)$ 

## Relationship between $K_a$ and $pK_a$

> [!table]- Strength Comparison
> | Acid Name | $K_a$ | $pK_a$ | Strength |
> | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
> | Hydrogen sulfate ion ($\ce{HSO4-}$) | $1.02 \times 10^{-2}$ | 1.99 | Stronger |
> | Nitrous acid ($\ce{HNO_{2}}$) | $\ce{7.1\times 10^{-4}}$ | 3.15 | |
> | Acetic acid ($\ce{CH3COOH}$) | $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ | 4.74 | |
> | Hypobromous acid ($\ce{HBrO}$) | $2.3\times 10^{-9}$ | 8.64 | |
> | Phenol ($\ce{C6H5OH}$) | $1.0 \times 10^{-10}$ | 10.00 | Weaker |
> > [!trend]- Trend
> > * As $K_a$ ||increases||, Acid Strength ||increases||.
> > * As $pK_a$ ||decreases||, Acid Strength ||increases||. 

## Sample Calculations

> [!sq]- Example 1: Acidic Buffer (ICE vs Henderson-Hasselbalch)
> A buffer solution contains $0.11\,M$ Sodium Acetate ($\ce{NaCH3COO}$) and $0.090\,M$ Acetic Acid ($\ce{CH3COOH}$). Calculate the pH. ($K_a = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$).
> > [!continue]- Method 1: ICE Table
> > $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + H2O <=> CH3COO-(aq) + H3O+(aq)}$
> >
> > | | $\ce{CH3COOH}$ | $\ce{CH3COO-}$ | $\ce{H3O+}$ |
> > | :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
> > | **I** | $0.090$ | $0.11$ | $0$ |
> > | **C** | $-x$ | $+x$ | $+x$ |
> > | **E** | $0.090-x$ | $0.11+x$ | $x$ |
> >
> > $1.8 \times 10^{-5} = \frac{(x)(0.11+x)}{0.090-x} \approx \frac{0.11x}{0.090}$
> > $x = 1.47 \times 10^{-5}\,M$.
> > $pH = -\log(1.47 \times 10^{-5}) = \enclose{box}{4.83}$. 
>
> > [!continue]- Method 2: Henderson-Hasselbalch
> > $pH = pK_a + \log \frac{[\ce{CH3COO-}]}{[\ce{CH3COOH}]}$
> > $pK_a = -\log(1.8 \times 10^{-5}) = 4.74$.
> > $pH = 4.74 + \log \left( \frac{0.11}{0.090} \right)$
> > $pH = 4.74 + 0.087 = \enclose{box}{4.83}$ 

> [!sq]- Example 2: Basic Buffer
> Calculate the pH of a buffer solution consisting of $0.30\,M$ $\ce{NH3}$ and $0.36\,M$ $\ce{NH4Cl}$. ($pK_b = 4.75$).
> > [!continue]- Method A: Using pOH
> > $pOH = pK_b + \log \frac{[\ce{NH4+}]}{[\ce{NH3}]}$
> > $pOH = 4.75 + \log \left( \frac{0.36}{0.30} \right)$
> > $pOH = 4.75 + 0.079 = 4.83$.
> > $pH = 14 - 4.83 = \enclose{box}{9.17}$ 
>
> > [!continue]- Method B: Using pH
> > $pK_a (\ce{NH4+}) = 14 - 4.75 = 9.25$.
> > $pH = pK_a + \log \frac{[\ce{NH3}]}{[\ce{NH4+}]}$ (Base/Acid)
> > $pH = 9.25 + \log \left( \frac{0.30}{0.36} \right)$
> > $pH = 9.25 + (-0.079) = \enclose{box}{9.17}$ 

> [!sq]- Example 3: Buffer Preparation
> In preparation of an acidic buffer with **pH 3.73**, a certain amount of sodium benzoate ($\ce{C6H5COONa}$) is added into $250.0\,mL$ of $0.15\,M$ benzoic acid ($\ce{C6H5COOH}$). Calculate the mass of sodium benzoate added.
> Given $K_a = 6.3 \times 10^{-5}$.
> > [!continue]- Step 1: Determine $pK_a$
> > $pK_a = -\log(6.3 \times 10^{-5}) = 4.20$.
>
> > [!continue]- Step 2: Use Henderson-Hasselbalch
> > $pH = pK_a + \log \frac{[\text{Salt}]}{[\text{Acid}]}$
> > $3.73 = 4.20 + \log \frac{[\text{Salt}]}{0.15}$
> > $-0.47 = \log \frac{[\text{Salt}]}{0.15}$
> > $10^{-0.47} = \frac{[\text{Salt}]}{0.15} \implies 0.3388 = \frac{[\text{Salt}]}{0.15}$.
> > $[\text{Salt}] = 0.0508\,M$.
>
> > [!continue]- Step 3: Calculate Moles and Mass
> > Moles $= M \times V = 0.0508 \times 0.250\,L = 0.0127\,mol$.
> > Molar Mass ($\ce{C6H5COONa}$) $\approx 144\,g/mol$.
> > Mass $= 0.0127 \times 144 = \enclose{box}{1.83\,g}$ 

## Exercises

> [!sq]- Exercise 1: HF/NaF Buffer
> Calculate the pH of a buffer solution which consists of $25\,g$ of $\ce{NaF}$ in $120\,mL$ of $2.0\,M$ $\ce{HF}$. ($K_a = 6.8 \times 10^{-4}$).
> > [!continue]- Step 1: Calculate Moles
> > Moles $\ce{NaF} = \frac{25\,g}{42\,g/mol} = 0.595\,mol$. (This is moles of $\ce{F-}$).
> > Moles $\ce{HF} = 2.0\,M \times 0.120\,L = 0.24\,mol$.
>
> > [!continue]- Step 2: Apply Formula
> > Since volume is constant, we can use moles directly in the log ratio.
> > $pH = pK_a + \log \frac{n(\ce{F-})}{n(\ce{HF})}$
> > $pK_a = -\log(6.8 \times 10^{-4}) = 3.17$.
> > $pH = 3.17 + \log \left( \frac{0.595}{0.24} \right)$
> > $pH = 3.17 + 0.39 = \enclose{box}{3.56}$

> [!sq]- Exercise 2: Adding Strong Base/Acid
> Using the buffer from Exercise 1:
> (a) If $2.50\,mL$ of $2.0\,M$ $\ce{NaOH}$ is added.
> (b) If $5\,mL$ of $1.5\,M$ $\ce{HCl}$ is added.
> > [!continue]- (a) Adding NaOH (Base)
> > **Reaction:** $\ce{HF + OH- -> F- + H2O}$ (Acid is consumed, Base increases).
> > * Moles $\ce{OH-} = 2.0 \times 0.0025 = 0.005\,mol$.
> > * New $\ce{HF} = 0.24 - 0.005 = 0.235\,mol$.
> > * New $\ce{F-} = 0.595 + 0.005 = 0.600\,mol$.
> >
> > **New pH:**
> > $pH = 3.17 + \log \left( \frac{0.600}{0.235} \right)$
> > $pH = 3.17 + 0.41 = \enclose{box}{3.58}$ (Slight increase).
>
> > [!continue]- (b) Adding HCl (Acid)
> > **Reaction:** $\ce{F- + H+ -> HF}$ (Base is consumed, Acid increases).
> > * Moles $\ce{H+} = 1.5 \times 0.005 = 0.0075\,mol$.
> > * New $\ce{F-} = 0.595 - 0.0075 = 0.5875\,mol$.
> > * New $\ce{HF} = 0.24 + 0.0075 = 0.2475\,mol$.
> >
> > **New pH:**
> > $pH = 3.17 + \log \left( \frac{0.5875}{0.2475} \right)$
> > $pH = 3.17 + 0.375 = \enclose{box}{3.55}$ (Slight decrease). 

# 4.6 Acid-Base Titration (Fundamentals)

## Titration Basics

> [!definition]- Titration
> A technique where a solution of **known concentration** (titrant) is added to a solution of **unknown concentration** (analyte) to determine its concentration.

> [!definition]- Equivalence Point
> The point in a titration where the number of moles of added base/acid is stoichiometrically $\color{orange}i$ to the moles of acid/base in the original solution.
> * It represents the theoretical completion of the reaction.
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > equal

> [!definition]- End Point
> The point in a titration where the **indicator** changes color.
> * It is an experimental approximation of the equivalence point.

## Methods for Measuring pH

> [!liststart]- Techniques
> > [!continue]- pH Indicators
> > Substances (weak acids) that change color over a specific pH range.
> > * Used for visual detection of the end point.
>
> > [!continue]- pH Meter
> > An electronic device used to measure pH continuously.
> > * **Technique:** pH is measured after each addition of titrant.
> > * **Titration Curve:** A plot of ||pH|| versus ||Volume of titrant added||.

## Calculation Strategies (Weak Acid - Strong Base)

> [!procedure]- Four Stages of Titration
> Consider titrating Weak Acid ($\ce{HA}$) with Strong Base ($\ce{OH-}$).
> > [!continue]- 1. Initial (No Base added)
> > * **Species:** Only Weak Acid ($\ce{HA}$).
> > * **Calculation:** Use $K_a$ and ICE table.
> > * **Formula:** $pH = -\log \sqrt{K_a \times [HA]_{init}}$ (approximation).
>
> > [!continue]- 2. Pre-Equivalence (Buffer Region)
> > * **Species:** Mixture of unreacted $\ce{HA}$ and formed $\ce{A-}$ (Salt).
> > * **Calculation:** This is a Buffer.
> > * **Formula:** Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation.
> >   $pH = pK_a + \log \left( \frac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{acid}]} \right)$
>
> > [!continue]- 3. At Equivalence Point
> > * **Species:** Only Salt ($\ce{A-}$). All acid has reacted.
> > * **Calculation:** Salt Hydrolysis.
> > * **Steps:**
> >     1. Calculate $[A^-] = \frac{\text{moles initial acid}}{V_{\text{total}}}$.
> >     2. Find $K_b = K_w / K_a$.
> >     3. Find $[OH^-] = \sqrt{K_b \times [A^-]}$.
> >     4. Calculate pOH, then pH.
>
> > [!continue]- 4. Post-Equivalence
> > * **Species:** Excess Strong Base ($\ce{OH-}$) and Salt.
> > * **Calculation:** pH is determined by excess strong base.
> > * **Steps:**
> >     1. Moles excess $\ce{OH-} = \text{Moles added} - \text{Moles acid}$.
> >     2. $[OH^-] = \frac{\text{Moles excess}}{V_{\text{total}}}$.
> >     3. Calculate pOH, then pH.

> > [!continue]- (ii) Change in pH after adding NaOH
> > **Scenario:** Add $0.01\,mol$ of $\ce{NaOH}$.
> > **Reaction:** $\ce{H2CO3 + OH- -> HCO3- + H2O}$.
> >
> > **Step 1: Initial Moles**
> > $n(\ce{H2CO3}) = 0.20 \times 1.50 = 0.30\,mol$.
> > $n(\ce{HCO3-}) = 2.75\,mol$.
> >
> > **Step 2: New Moles**
> > $\ce{OH-}$ consumes Acid, produces Base.
> > $n(\ce{H2CO3})_{new} = 0.30 - 0.01 = 0.29\,mol$.
> > $n(\ce{HCO3-})_{new} = 2.75 + 0.01 = 2.76\,mol$.
> >
> > **Step 3: New pH**
> > $pH_{new} = 6.37 + \log \left( \frac{2.76}{0.29} \right)$
> > $pH_{new} = 6.37 + \log(9.517) = 6.37 + 0.98 = 7.35$.
> >
> > **Step 4: Change**
> > $\Delta pH = 7.35 - 7.33 = \enclose{box}{+0.02}$
