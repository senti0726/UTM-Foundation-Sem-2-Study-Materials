# 5.1 Definitions of Redox Reaction

> [!definition]- Electrochemistry
> The study of relationships between $\color{orange}i$ where it involves$\color{orange}ii$.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > electrical and chemical energy
>
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > oxidation-reduction reactions
## Redox Reactions

> [!definition]- Redox Reaction
> Reactions that involve the **transfer of electrons** from one atom to another.
> * **Note:** Oxidation and reduction occur ||simultaneously||.

> [!compare]- Oxidation vs Reduction
> > [!continue]- Oxidation
> > * **Process:** ||Losing|| electrons.
> > * **Oxidation Number:** ||Increases||.
> > * **Half-Reaction:** Electrons are on the ||product|| side.
>
> > [!continue]- Reduction
> > * **Process:** ||Gaining|| electrons.
> > * **Oxidation Number:** ||Decreases||.
> > * **Half-Reaction:** Electrons are on the ||reactant|| side.

> [!sq]- Example: Magnesium and Hydrogen
> Reaction: $\ce{Mg(s) + 2H+(aq) -> Mg^{2+}(aq) + H2(g)}$
> > [!continue]- Oxidation (Magnesium)
> > $\ce{Mg(s) -> Mg^{2+}(aq) + 2e-}$
> > * Mg ||loses|| 2 electrons.
> > * Oxidation number increases from $0$ to $+2$.
> > * Mg is ||oxidized||.
>
> > [!continue]- Reduction (Hydrogen)
> > $\ce{2H+(aq) + 2e- -> H2(g)}$
> > * $\ce{H+}$ ||gains|| 2 electrons.
> > * Oxidation number decreases from $+1$ to $0$.
> > * $\ce{H+}$ is ||reduced||.
## Oxidizing and Reducing Agents

> [!definition]- Oxidizing Agent
> A species that causes the **oxidation** of another species.
> * It contains the element that is **reduced**.
> * It ||gains|| electrons.
> * **Examples:** $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{F2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{KMnO4}$, $\ce{HNO3}$.

> [!definition]- Reducing Agent
> A species that causes the **reduction** of another species.
> * It contains the element that is **oxidized**.
> * It ||loses|| electrons.
> * **Examples:** $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{C}$ (coke), $\ce{Na}$, $\ce{Fe}$, $\ce{Zn}$.

> [!liststart]- A redox reaction can be broken into two half-reactions
> > [!continue]- Oxidation half-rxn
> > $\ce{Mg(s)->Mg^{2+}(aq) +2e-}$
> 
> > [!continue]- Reduction half-rxn
> > $\ce{2H+(aq) +2e-->H_{2}(g)}$
> 
> > [!continue]- Overall reaction
> > $\ce{Mg(s) +2H+(aq)->Mg^{2+}(aq) +H_{2}(g)}$

> [!sq]- Summary Example
> Reaction: $\ce{2Na(s) + Cl2(g) -> 2NaCl(s)}$
> > [!continue]- Sodium ($\ce{Na}$)
> > * **Action:** Loses electron ($\ce{Na -> Na+ + e-}$).
> > * **Status:** Oxidized.
> > * **Role:** ||Reducing Agent||.
>
> > [!continue]- Chlorine ($\ce{Cl2}$)
> > * **Action:** Gains electron ($\ce{Cl2 + 2e- -> 2Cl-}$).
> > * **Status:** Reduced.
> > * **Role:** ||Oxidizing Agent||.
# 5.2 Oxidation Number

## Definition

> [!definition]- Oxidation Number
> Also known as **Oxidation State**.
> * It refers to the number of electrons transferred, shared, or otherwise involved in chemical bonds.
> * It is a **positive or negative number** assigned to an atom to show how it is bonded.

## Rules for Assigning Oxidation Numbers


> [!liststart]- General Rules
> > [!continue]- 1. Free Elements
> > Atoms or molecules of an element in its free state have an oxidation number of ||0||.
> > * Examples: $\ce{Na} = 0$, $\ce{Cl2} = 0$.
>
> > [!continue]- 2. Monatomic Ions
> > Ions consisting of only one atom have an oxidation number equal to their ||charge||.
> > * Examples: $\ce{Na+} = +1$, $\ce{Cl-} = -1$.

> [!liststart]- Summation Rules
> > [!continue]- 3. Neutral Compounds
> > The sum of the oxidation states of all atoms in a neutral compound is ||0||.
> > * Example: $\ce{NaCl}$ ($+1 + (-1) = 0$).
>
> > [!continue]- 4. Polyatomic Ions
> > The sum of the oxidation states of all atoms in a polyatomic ion equals the ||charge on the ion||.
> > * Example: $\ce{NO3-}$ ($\text{N} + 3(-2) = -1 \implies \text{N} = +5$).

> [!liststart]- Element-Specific Rules
> 
> > [!continue]- 5. Group I and II Metals
> > > [!continue]- Group I Metals
> > > Always have an oxidation state of ||+1|| in all compounds.
> > > * Example: $\ce{Na} = +1$ in $\ce{NaCl}$.
> >
> > > [!continue]- Group II Metals
> > > Always have an oxidation state of ||+2|| in all compounds.
> > > * Example: $\ce{Mg} = +2$ in $\ce{MgCl2}$.
>
> > [!continue]- 6. Hydrogen ($\ce{H}$)
> > > [!continue]- Covalent Hydrides ($\ce{H}$ + Non-metal)
> > > Oxidation number is ||+1||.
> > > * Examples: $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{NH3}$.
> >
> > > [!continue]- Ionic Hydrides ($\ce{H}$ + Metal)
> > > Oxidation number is ||-1||.
> > > * Examples: $\ce{NaH}$, $\ce{MgH2}$, $\ce{CaH2}$.
>
> > [!continue]- 7. Oxygen ($\ce{O}$)
> > > [!continue]- Oxides (General case)
> > > Oxidation number is ||-2||.
> > > * Examples: $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{MgO}$.
> >
> > > [!continue]- Peroxides
> > > Oxidation number is ||-1||.
> > > * Examples: $\ce{H2O2}$, $\ce{Na2O2}$, $\ce{BaO2}$.
> >
> > > [!continue]- With Fluorine ($\ce{F}$)
> > > Since $\ce{F}$ is more electronegative, Oxygen is ||+2||.
> > > * Example: $\ce{F2O}$.
>
> > [!continue]- 8. Group VII (Halogens)
> > > [!continue]- Fluorine ($\ce{F}$)
> > > Always ||-1|| in all compounds.
> >
> > > [!continue]- Other Halogens ($\ce{Cl}, \ce{Br}, \ce{I}$)
> > > Usually ||-1|| when they occur as halide ions.
> > > * **Exception:** When combined with Oxygen (Oxyanions).
> > > * In these cases, the halogen has a ||positive|| oxidation number.
> > > * Example: $\ce{HClO4}$ ($\ce{Cl} = +7$).

# 5.2 Oxidation Number (Calculations & Application)

## Calculating Oxidation Numbers

> [!sq]- Example 1: Polyatomic Ion with Fraction
> Determine the oxidation states of all atoms in the **propanoate ion**, $\ce{C3H5O2-}$.
> > [!continue]- Calculation
> > Let $C$ be the oxidation state of Carbon.
> > * $\ce{H} = +1$ (attached to non-metal).
> > * $\ce{O} = -2$.
> > * Sum of oxidation states = Charge of ion ($-1$).
> >
> > Equation: $3(C) + 5(+1) + 2(-2) = -1$
> > $3C + 5 - 4 = -1$
> > $3C + 1 = -1$
> > $3C = -2$
> > $\enclose{box}{C = -2/3}$
> >
> > **Note:** Unlike ionic charges, oxidation states ||can be fractions||.

> [!sq]- Example 2: Sodium Persulfate
> Calculate the oxidation state of Sulfur in $\ce{Na2S2O8}$.
> 
> > [!continue]- Calculation
> > * $\ce{Na} = +1$ (Group I metal).
> > * $\ce{O} = -2$.
> > * Sum = $0$ (Neutral compound).
> >
> > Equation: $2(+1) + 2(S) + 8(-2) = 0$
> > $2 + 2S - 16 = 0$
> > $2S = 14$
> > $\enclose{box}{S = +7}$

> [!sq]- Example 3: Dichromate Ion
> Calculate the oxidation state of Chromium in $\ce{Cr2O7^{2-}}$.
> 
> > [!continue]- Calculation
> > * $\ce{O} = -2$.
> > * Sum = $-2$.
> >
> > Equation: $2(Cr) + 7(-2) = -2$
> > $2Cr - 14 = -2$
> > $2Cr = 12$
> > $\enclose{box}{Cr = +6}$

> > * **Element Reduced:** $\ce{Mn}$ (in $\ce{MnO4-}$).
> > * **Oxidizing Agent:** $\ce{MnO4-}$ (The species that contains the reduced element).

# 5.3 Balancing Redox Reactions (Half-Reaction Method)

> [!info]- General Concept
> The half-reaction method breaks a redox reaction into two separate parts:
> 1.  **Oxidation half-reaction** (Loss of electrons).
> 2.  **Reduction half-reaction** (Gain of electrons).
> * **Goal:** To balance atoms and charge separately, then recombine them so that electrons lost equal electrons gained.

## Balancing in Acidic Solution

> [!procedure]- Steps for Acidic Solution
> 
> 1.  **Assign Oxidation States:** Identify the element oxidized and reduced.
> 2.  **Separate:** Write the oxidation and reduction half-reactions.
> 3.  **Balance Atoms (Mass):**
>     * Balance all elements **except** $\ce{H}$ and $\ce{O}$.
>     * Balance $\ce{O}$ by adding **$\ce{H2O}$**.
>     * Balance $\ce{H}$ by adding **$\ce{H+}$**.
> 4.  **Balance Charge:** Add electrons ($\ce{e-}$) to the more positive side.
> 5.  **Equalize Electrons:** Multiply half-reactions by integers so electrons match.
> 6.  **Combine:** Add half-reactions and cancel common species.

> [!sq]- Example 5: Acidic Solution
> **Reaction:** $\ce{Fe^{2+} + MnO4- -> Fe^{3+} + Mn^{2+}}$
> > [!continue]- 1. Assign Oxidation States
> > * $\ce{Fe^{2+}} \to (+2)$
> > * $\ce{MnO4-}: \text{Mn} + 4(-2) = -1 \implies \text{Mn} = (+7)$
> > * $\ce{Fe^{3+}} \to (+3)$
> > * $\ce{Mn^{2+}} \to (+2)$
>
> > [!continue]- 2. Separate Half-Reactions
> > * **Oxidation:** $\ce{Fe^{2+} -> Fe^{3+}}$ (Loss of electrons).
> > * **Reduction:** $\ce{MnO4- -> Mn^{2+}}$ (Gain of electrons).
>
> > [!continue]- 3. Balance Atoms (Mass)
> > * **Oxidation:** Fe is already balanced ($1 \to 1$).
> > * **Reduction:**
> >     * Balance $\ce{Mn}$: $1 \to 1$ (Balanced).
> >     * Balance $\ce{O}$: Reactant has 4 O. Add **$4\ce{H2O}$** to product.
> >       $\ce{MnO4- -> Mn^{2+} + 4H2O}$
> >     * Balance $\ce{H}$: Product has $8\ce{H}$ ($4 \times 2$). Add **$8\ce{H+}$** to reactant.
> >       $\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ -> Mn^{2+} + 4H2O}$
>
> > [!continue]- 4. Balance Charge (with Electrons)
> > * **Oxidation:** $+2 \to +3$.
> >     * Add $1\ce{e-}$ to product side.
> >     * $\ce{Fe^{2+} -> Fe^{3+} + 1e-}$
> > * **Reduction:** Left side ($8\ce{H+} + \ce{MnO4-}$) = $+8 - 1 = +7$. Right side ($\ce{Mn^{2+}}$) = $+2$.
> >     * Difference is $5$. Add **$5\ce{e-}$** to reactant side.
> >     * $\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5e- -> Mn^{2+} + 4H2O}$
>
> > [!continue]- 5. Equalize Electrons
> > * Oxidation has $1\ce{e-}$. Reduction has $5\ce{e-}$.
> > * Multiply Oxidation half-reaction by **5**.
> >     * $5(\ce{Fe^{2+} -> Fe^{3+} + 1e-})$
> >     * $\implies 5\ce{Fe^{2+} -> 5Fe^{3+} + 5e-}$
>
> > [!continue]- 6. Add and Cancel
> > * **Reactants:** $5\ce{Fe^{2+}} + \ce{MnO4-} + 8\ce{H+} + 5\ce{e-}$
> > * **Products:** $5\ce{Fe^{3+}} + 5\ce{e-} + \ce{Mn^{2+}} + 4\ce{H2O}$
> > * **Cancel:** $5\ce{e-}$ from both sides.
> > * **Final Equation:**
> >   $\ce{5Fe^{2+} + MnO4- + 8H+ -> 5Fe^{3+} + Mn^{2+} + 4H2O}$

> [!sq]- Exercise (Acidic)
> Balance: $\ce{Sn^{2+}(aq) + IO3-(aq) -> Sn^{4+}(aq) + I-(aq)}$
> > [!continue]- Solution
> > 1.  **Oxidation:** $\ce{Sn^{2+} -> Sn^{4+} + 2e-}$.
> > 2.  **Reduction:** $\ce{IO3- -> I-}$.
> >     * Balance O: $\ce{IO3- -> I- + 3H2O}$.
> >     * Balance H: $\ce{IO3- + 6H+ -> I- + 3H2O}$.
> >     * Balance Charge ($+5 \to -1$): $\ce{IO3- + 6H+ + 6e- -> I- + 3H2O}$.
> > 3.  **Combine:** Multiply Oxidation by $3$ (to get $6\ce{e-}$).
> >     $3\ce{Sn^{2+}} + \ce{IO3-} + 6\ce{H+} \to 3\ce{Sn^{4+}} + \ce{I-} + 3\ce{H2O}$.

## Balancing in Basic Solution

> [!info]- Concept
> Basic solutions contain $\ce{OH-}$ instead of $\ce{H+}$.
> * **Strategy:** Balance as if in **acidic** solution first, then convert to basic.

> [!procedure]- Procedure (Page 31)
> Basic solutions contain $\ce{OH-}$ ions instead of $\ce{H+}$. The strategy is to balance as if acidic, then "neutralize" the protons.
>
> > [!continue]- Step 1-3: Standard Acidic Method
> > * Assign oxidation states.
> > * Separate into half-reactions.
> > * Balance atoms (Mass):
> >     * Balance elements other than H and O.
> >     * Balance **O** by adding $\ce{H2O}$.
> >     * Balance **H** by adding $\ce{H+}$.
>
> > [!continue]- Step 4: Convert to Basic
> > * Neutralize $\ce{H+}$ by adding **$\ce{OH-}$** to **both sides** of the equation.
> > * **Key Rule:** For every $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ on the same side, combine them to form **$\ce{H2O}$**.
> > * Cancel excess $\ce{H2O}$ molecules appearing on both sides.
>
> > [!continue]- Step 5: Balance Charge
> > * Balance the charge by adding electrons ($\ce{e-}$).
>
> > [!continue]- Step 6-7: Finalize
> > * Multiply half-reactions to equalize electron count.
> > * Add half-reactions and cancel common species.
> > * Check atom count and total charge.

## Detailed Example: Basic Solution

> [!sq]- Example 6: Chromate and Iodide
> **Reaction:** $\ce{I- + CrO4^{2-} -> I2 + Cr^{3+}}$ (in basic solution)
> > [!continue]- Step 1: Half-Reactions
> > * **Oxidation:** $\ce{I- -> I2}$ (Iodine oxidation state $-1 \to 0$).
> > * **Reduction:** $\ce{CrO4^{2-} -> Cr^{3+}}$ (Chromium oxidation state $+6 \to +3$).
>
> > [!continue]- Step 2: Balance Atoms (Acidic Method first)
> > * **Oxidation:** $2\ce{I- -> I2}$.
> > * **Reduction:**
> >     * Balance O: Add $4\ce{H2O}$. $\ce{CrO4^{2-} -> Cr^{3+} + 4H2O}$.
> >     * Balance H: Add $8\ce{H+}$. $\ce{CrO4^{2-} + 8H+ -> Cr^{3+} + 4H2O}$. 
>
> > [!continue]- Step 3: Convert to Basic
> > * **Rule:** Neutralize $\ce{H+}$ by adding $\ce{OH-}$ to **both sides**.
> > * Add $8\ce{OH-}$:
> >     $\ce{CrO4^{2-} + \underbrace{8H+ + 8OH-}_{8H2O} -> Cr^{3+} + 4H2O + 8OH-}$
> > * **Simplify Water:** Cancel $4\ce{H2O}$ from both sides.
> >     $\ce{CrO4^{2-} + 4H2O -> Cr^{3+} + 8OH-}$
>
> > [!continue]- Step 4: Balance Charge & Electrons
> > * **Oxidation:** $2\ce{I- -> I2 + 2e-}$ (Charge $-2 \to 0$).
> > * **Reduction:** $\ce{CrO4^{2-} + 4H2O + 3e- -> Cr^{3+} + 8OH-}$ (Charge $-2 \to +3-8 = -5$).
>
> > [!continue]- Step 5: Combine
> > * Multiply Oxidation by $3$ ($6\ce{e-}$).
> > * Multiply Reduction by $2$ ($6\ce{e-}$).
> > * **Add:**
> >     $6\ce{I-} + 2\ce{CrO4^{2-}} + 8\ce{H2O} + 6\ce{e-} \to 3\ce{I2} + 6\ce{e-} + 2\ce{Cr^{3+}} + 16\ce{OH-}$
> > * **Final Equation:**
> >     $\ce{6I-(aq) + 2CrO4^{2-}(aq) + 8H2O(l) -> 3I2(s) + 2Cr^{3+}(aq) + 16OH-(aq)}$

> > * Balance H (Acidic): $\ce{MnO4- + 4H+ -> MnO2 + 2H2O}$.
> > * Convert to Basic (+4$\ce{OH-}$): $\ce{MnO4- + 4H2O -> MnO2 + 2H2O + 4OH-}$.
> > * Simplify: $\ce{MnO4- + 2H2O -> MnO2 + 4OH-}$.
> > * Charge: $\ce{MnO4- + 2H2O + 3e- -> MnO2 + 4OH-}$. 
>
> > [!continue]- Step 4: Combine
> > * Multiply Ox by $3$ ($6\ce{e-}$).
> > * Multiply Red by $2$ ($6\ce{e-}$).
> > * **Add:**
> >     $3(\ce{CN-} + 2\ce{OH-}) + 2(\ce{MnO4-} + 2\ce{H2O}) \to \dots$
> >     $3\ce{CN-} + 6\ce{OH-} + 2\ce{MnO4-} + 4\ce{H2O} \to 3\ce{CNO-} + 3\ce{H2O} + 2\ce{MnO2} + 8\ce{OH-}$
> > * **Simplify (Cancel common species):**
> >     $3\ce{CN-} + 2\ce{MnO4-} + \ce{H2O} \to 3\ce{CNO-} + 2\ce{MnO2} + 2\ce{OH-}$

# 5.4 The Voltaic Cells

## Introduction and Components

> [!definition]- Voltaic Cell (Galvanic Cell)
> An electrochemical cell that produces $\color{orange}i$ current due to the electrical potential of two **spontaneous** redox reactions.
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > electrical

> [!liststart]- Key Components
> > [!continue]- Half-Cells
> > Two separate containers containing electrolyte solutions.
> > * One for the oxidation reaction.
> > * One for the reduction reaction.
>
> > [!continue]- Electrodes
> > Solid conductors dipped in the electrolyte solutions.
> > * Connect the half-cells to an external circuit (e.g., voltmeter).
>
> > [!continue]- Salt Bridge
> > An inverted U-tube containing an inert electrolyte (e.g., $\ce{KCl}$, $\ce{KNO3}$) in agar gel.
> > * **Function 1:** Completes the electrical circuit.
> > * **Function 2:** Maintains ||electrical neutrality|| in the half-cells.

## Cell Operation
> [!picture]- Spontaneous redox reaction (Voltaic Cells) of $\ce{ZnSO4}$ solution and $\ce{CuSO4}$ solution
> ![[CHAPTER 5 2026-02-09 00.33.53.excalidraw]]
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > Anode (oxidation)
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > Cathode (reduction)
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iv$
> > $\ce{Zn}$ is oxidised to $\ce{Zn^{2+}}$ at anode.
> 
> > [!blank]- $\color{orange}iv$
> > $\ce{Cu^{2+}}$ is oxidised to $\ce{Cu}$ at cathode

> [!compare]- Anode vs Cathode
> > [!continue]- Anode (Negative Terminal)
> > * **Process:** ||Oxidation|| (Loss of electrons).
> > * **Electron Flow:** Electrons ||leave|| the anode toward the external circuit.
> > * **Mass:** The metal electrode may dissolve (mass decreases) as it turns into ions.
>
> > [!continue]- Cathode (Positive Terminal)
> > * **Process:** ||Reduction|| (Gain of electrons).
> > * **Electron Flow:** Electrons ||enter|| the cathode from the external circuit.
> > * **Mass:** Metal ions may deposit on the electrode (mass increases).

> [!procedure]- Flow of Ions (Salt Bridge)
> To prevent the buildup of charge and maintain neutrality:
> > [!continue]- Anions (Negative Ions)
> > Flow toward the **$\color{orange}i$** half-cell.
> > * Reason: To neutralize the excess positive charge formed by oxidation ($\ce{M -> M+ + e-}$).
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}i$
> > > Anode
>
> > [!continue]- Cations (Positive Ions)
> > Flow toward the **$\color{orange}ii$** half-cell.
> > * Reason: To neutralize the excess negative charge left as positive ions are consumed ($\ce{M+ + e- -> M}$).
> > > [!blank]- $\color{orange}ii$
> > > Cathode

# 5.5 Cell Potential ($E^\circ_{cell}$)
> [!definition]- Potential difference
> The difference in ||potential energy|| between ||the reactants and the product||
> > [!unit]- Unit conversion to energy
> > $1V=1J\,C^{-1}$ (energy per Coulomb of charge $^{-1}$)

> [!definition]- Electromotive force (EMF)
> Amount of force pushing the electrons through the wire 

> [!formula]- For a standard cell, the potential is
> ${E^{o}}_\text{cell}={E^{o}}_\text{red}(\text{cathode})-{E^{o}}_\text{red}(\text{anode})$
> > [!unit]- ${E^{o}}_\text{cell}$
> > Electric potential difference in $J\,C^{-1}$
> 
> > [!unit]- ${E^{o}}_\text{red}(\text{cathode})$
> > Reduction potential of the cathode in $V$
> 
> > [!unit]- ${E^{o}}_\text{red}(\text{anode})$
> > Reduction potential of the anode in $J\,C^{-1}$

> [!continue]- Spontaneous redox reaction
> A redox reaction that has a ||positive cell potential $({E^{o}}_\text{cell})$|| is spontaneous (favour products)
> > [!continue]- If ${E^{o}}_\text{cell}$ is negative, the ||reverse reaction is spontaneous||.
> > > [!continue]- $E_\text{cell}>0.0V$
> > > The forward reaction is spontaneous (Voltaic or Galvanic cell aka ||Battery||)
> > 
> > > [!continue]- $E_\text{cell}=0.0V$
> > > There is no net flow of electrons because the system is ||at equilibrium||.
> > 
> > > [!continue]- $E_\text{cell}<0.0V$
> > > The reverse reaction is spontaneous (Electrolytic cell)

> [!definition]- Cell potential
> The difference in potential energy between the anode and the cathode in a voltaic cell

> [!continue]- When two half-cells are connected,
>  the electron will so that the half-reaction with ||the stronger tendency|| will occur.

> [!info]- Standard emf, ${E^{o}}_\text{cell}$
> > [!continue]- temperature
> > $25^{\circ}C$
> 
> > [!continue]- pressure
> > $1\,atm$ (for gases)
> 
> > [!continue]- Concentration
> > $1M$ concentration of solution
> 
> > [!continue]- Sum of the cell potentials for
> > the half-reactions

> > $\ce{Cr^{2+}(aq) +2e- ->Cr(s)}\hspace{2em}{E^{o}}_\text{red}=-0.91V$
> > $\ce{Ag+(aq) +e- ->Ag(s)}\hspace{2em}{E^{o}}_\text{red}=+0.8V$
> > Oxidation: $\ce{Cr(s)->Cr^{2+}(aq) +2e-}$
> > Reduction: $\ce{Ag+(aq) +e- ->Ag(s)}$
> > ![[CHAPTER 5 2026-02-09 09.12.09.excalidraw]]
> 
> > [!continue]- $ii.$ Write the half reactions and the overall reaction
> > $\underline{\text{Half equation}}$
> > Oxidation: $\ce{Cr(s)->Cr^{2+}(aq) +2e-}$
> > Reduction: $\ce{Ag+(aq) +e- ->Ag(s)}$
> > $\underline{\text{Overall equation}}$
> > $\ce{Cr(s) +2Ag+(aq)->Cr^{2+}(aq) +2Ag(s)}$

> [!sq]- Example 2: Predicting Spontaneity
> Will silver ions ($\ce{Ag+}$) react with metallic copper ($\ce{Cu}$)?
> **Reaction:** $\ce{2Ag+(aq) + Cu(s) -> 2Ag(s) + Cu^{2+}(aq)}$
> > [!continue]- Step 1: Identify Half-Reactions
> > * **Cathode (Reduction):** $\ce{Ag+ + e- -> Ag}$ ($E^\circ = +0.80\,V$)
> > * **Anode (Oxidation):** $\ce{Cu -> Cu^{2+} + 2e-}$ ($E^\circ = +0.34\,V$)
>
> > [!continue]- Step 2: Calculate Potential
> > $E^\circ_{cell} = (+0.80) - (+0.34)$
> > $E^\circ_{cell} = \enclose{box}{+0.46\,V}$
> > * Since potential is positive, the reaction ||will occur|| (Spontaneous).

> [!sq]- Example 3: Non-Spontaneous Reaction
> Will copper ions ($\ce{Cu^{2+}}$) react with metallic silver ($\ce{Ag}$)?
> **Reaction:** $\ce{Cu^{2+}(aq) + 2Ag(s) -> Cu(s) + 2Ag+(aq)}$
> > [!continue]- Analysis
> > * **Cathode:** $\ce{Cu^{2+} -> Cu}$ ($+0.34\,V$)
> > * **Anode:** $\ce{Ag -> Ag+}$ ($+0.80\,V$)
> > * $E^\circ_{cell} = 0.34 - 0.80 = \enclose{box}{-0.46\,V}$
> > * Reaction is ||non-spontaneous||.

# 5.6 Nernst Equation

> [!info]- Non-standard cells (Concentration Cells)
> For the generic cell reaction:
> $aA + bB \rightleftharpoons cC + dD$
> The potential of the non-standard cell is calculated using the Nernst Equation.

> [!formula]- The Nernst Equation
> $E_{cell} = E^\circ_{cell} - \frac{RT}{nF} \ln Q$
> 
> > [!continue]- At standard temperature ($25^\circ C$ or $298K$):
> > $E_{cell} = E^\circ_{cell} - \frac{0.0592}{n} \log Q$
>
> > [!unit]- $E_{cell}$
> > The potential of the non-standard cell ($V$)
>
> > [!unit]- $E^\circ_{cell}$
> > The standard cell potential ($V$)
>
> > [!unit]- $n$
> > The number of moles of electrons exchanged
>
> > [!unit]- $Q$
> > The reaction quotient: $\frac{[C]^c [D]^d}{[A]^a [B]^b}$
>
> > [!unit]- $F$
> > Faraday's constant ($96,485 \, C/mol \, e^-$ or $9.65 \times 10^4 \, J/V \cdot mol \, e^-$)

> [!compare]- Relationship between $Q$ and $E_{cell}$
> > [!continue]- When $Q < 1$
> > $[reactant] > [product]$
> > $\ln Q < 0$
> > $\therefore E_{cell} > E^\circ_{cell}$
>
> > [!continue]- When $Q = 1$
> > $[reactant] = [product]$
> > $\ln Q = 0$
> > $\therefore E_{cell} = E^\circ_{cell}$
>
> > [!continue]- When $Q > 1$
> > $[reactant] < [product]$
> > $\ln Q > 0$
> > $\therefore E_{cell} < E^\circ_{cell}$

## Sample Problems

> [!sq]- Sample Problem 1: Calculating EMF
> Calculate the emf of the following cell at $25^\circ C$.
> $\ce{Cu(s) | Cu^{2+}(0.03 M) || Ag+(0.002 M) | Ag(s)}$
> Given standard potentials (from memory/table): $E^\circ(\ce{Ag}) = +0.80V$, $E^\circ(\ce{Cu}) = +0.34V$.
> 
>
> > [!continue]- Step 1: Redox Half Equations
> > Oxidation: $\ce{Cu(s) -> Cu^{2+}(aq) + 2e-}$
> > Reduction: $\ce{2Ag+(aq) + 2e- -> 2Ag(s)}$
> > Overall: $\ce{Cu(s) + 2Ag+(aq) -> Cu^{2+}(aq) + 2Ag(s)}$
> > $n = 2$ (electrons transferred)
>
> > [!continue]- Step 2: Calculate $E^\circ_{cell}$
> > $E^\circ_{cell} = E^\circ_{red}(cat) - E^\circ_{red}(an)$
> > $E^\circ_{cell} = 0.80 - 0.34 = 0.46 \, V$
>
> > [!continue]- Step 3: $Q$ Expression
> > $Q = \frac{[\ce{Cu^{2+}}]}{[\ce{Ag+}]^2}$
> > Note: Solids ($\ce{Cu}$, $\ce{Ag}$) are omitted.
>
> > [!continue]- Step 4: Nernst Calculation
> > $E_{cell} = 0.46 - \frac{0.0592}{2} \log \frac{0.03}{(0.002)^2}$
> > $E_{cell} = 0.46 - \frac{0.0592}{2} \log (7500)$
> > $E_{cell} = \enclose{box}{0.345 \, V}$

## Nernst Equation Applications

> [!info]- Equilibrium Constant ($K_c$)
> At equilibrium, there is **no net flow** of electrons.
> * $E_{cell} = 0$
> * $Q = K_c$
>
> **Formula:**
> $0 = E^\circ_{cell} - \frac{0.0592}{n} \log K_c$
> $\implies E^\circ_{cell} = \frac{0.0592}{n} \log K_c$

> [!sq]- Sample Problem 2: Finding $K_c$
> Calculate the $K_c$ for the reaction between silver nitrate and metallic zinc:
> $\ce{2Ag+(aq) + Zn(s) \rightleftharpoons Zn^{2+}(aq) + 2Ag(s)}$
> Given: $E^\circ(\ce{Ag+}) = 0.80 \, V$, $E^\circ(\ce{Zn^{2+}}) = -0.76 \, V$.
> Is the reaction complete or incomplete?
>
> > [!continue]- Step 1: Half Reactions & $E^\circ_{cell}$
> > Ox: $\ce{Zn -> Zn^{2+} + 2e-}$
> > Red: $\ce{2Ag+ + 2e- -> 2Ag}$
> > $E^\circ_{cell} = 0.80 - (-0.76) = 1.56 \, V$
> > $n = 2$
>
> > [!continue]- Step 2: Calculate $K_c$
> > $0 = 1.56 - \frac{0.0592}{2} \log K_c$
> > $\log K_c = \frac{1.56 \times 2}{0.0592} \approx 52.7$
> > $K_c = \enclose{box}{5.04 \times 10^{52}}$
>
> > [!continue]- Conclusion
> > The reaction is **complete** because the extremely large value of $K_c$ indicates it favors the product.

> [!info]- Solubility Product ($K_{sp}$)
> The $K_{sp}$ of a sparingly soluble salt can be determined by measuring the $E_{cell}$ and applying the Nernst equation.

> [!sq]- Sample Problem 3: Finding $K_{sp}$
> **Anode:** Silver metal immersed in $1.0 \, M \, \ce{Cl-}$ solution with excess $\ce{AgCl(s)}$.
> **Cathode:** Standard $\ce{Ag}$ half-cell ($\ce{Ag+}$ $1.0 \, M$).
> Cell: $\ce{Ag(s) | AgCl(s) | Cl-(1M) || Ag+(1.0M) | Ag(s)}$
> Calculate $K_{sp}$ of $\ce{AgCl}$.
> Given:
> Anode rxn: $\ce{Ag(s) + Cl- -> AgCl(s) + e-}$ ($E^\circ_{red} = +0.22 V$)
> Cathode rxn: $\ce{Ag+ + e- -> Ag(s)}$ ($E^\circ_{red} = +0.80 V$)
>
> > [!continue]- Step 1: Cell Potential Calculation
> > $E^\circ_{cell} = 0.80 - 0.22 = 0.58 \, V$
> > $n = 1$
>
> > [!continue]- Step 2: Nernst Setup for Equilibrium
> > At equilibrium (saturated solution):
> > $0 = 0.58 - \frac{0.0592}{1} \log \frac{1}{[\ce{Ag+}][\ce{Cl-}]}$
> > Using standard potentials to find the equilibrium constant ($K_{sp}$):
> > $K_{sp} = [\ce{Ag+}][\ce{Cl-}]$
> > Calculation result shown: $\enclose{box}{1.59 \times 10^{-10}}$

> [!info]- pH Determination
> The pH of an acidic solution in the cathode compartment of a SHE half-cell can be determined by applying the Nernst equation.

> [!sq]- Sample Problem 4: Finding pH
> A voltaic cell consists of a $\ce{Zn}$ and a $\ce{H2}$ half-cell:
> $\ce{Zn(s) | Zn^{2+}(0.90 M) || H+(aq) | H2(1.0 atm) | Pt}$
> The cell potential is $0.54 \, V$ and $E^\circ_{red}$ of $\ce{Zn^{2+}}$ is $-0.76 \, V$.
> Calculate the pH of the acid used in the SHE half-cell.
> 
>
> > [!continue]- Step 1: Overall Equation & $E^\circ_{cell}$
> > Ox: $\ce{Zn -> Zn^{2+} + 2e-}$
> > Red: $\ce{2H+ + 2e- -> H2}$
> > Overall: $\ce{Zn(s) + 2H+(aq) -> Zn^{2+}(aq) + H2(g)}$
> > $E^\circ_{cell} = 0.00 - (-0.76) = 0.76 \, V$
> > $n = 2$
>
> > [!continue]- Step 2: Nernst Equation
> > $0.54 = 0.76 - \frac{0.0592}{2} \log \frac{[\ce{Zn^{2+}}] P_{\ce{H2}}}{[\ce{H+}]^2}$
> > $0.54 = 0.76 - 0.0296 \log \frac{0.90(1)}{[\ce{H+}]^2}$
>
> > [!continue]- Step 3: Solve for $[\ce{H+}]$
> > Solving the algebra...
> > $[\ce{H+}] = 1.823 \times 10^{-4} \, M$
>
> > [!continue]- Step 4: Calculate pH
> > $pH = -\log [\ce{H+}]$
> > $pH = \enclose{box}{3.74}$

# 5.7 Voltaic Cell in Application

## Corrosion of Iron (Formation of Rust)

> [!definition]- Corrosion
> A chemical process that causes a destructive effect on metals by electrochemical reaction with its environment.
> * **Effect:** Rust tarnishes the surface causing the metal to become weak and fragile.

> [!procedure]- Mechanism of Rust Formation
> Requires the presence of **Oxygen ($\ce{O2}$)** and **Water ($\ce{H2O}$)**.
> 
> > [!continue]- Anode (Oxidation)
> > $\ce{Fe(s) -> Fe^{2+}(aq) + 2e^{-}}$
> > ${E^{o}}_\text{red} = -0.44V$
>
> > [!continue]- Cathode (Reduction)
> > $\ce{O2(g) + 4H+(aq) + 4e^{-} -> 2H2O(l)}$
> > ${E^{o}}_\text{red} = 1.23V$
>
> > [!continue]- Why Iron Oxidizes?
> > The reduction of oxygen is ||favored|| (more positive $E^\circ$) over the reduction of iron.
> > * Hence, iron will preferentially ||oxidize||.
>
> > [!continue]- Final Product
> > The $\ce{Fe^{2+}}$ formed is oxidized further to $\ce{Fe^{3+}}$.
> > * Forms hydrated iron (III) oxide: $\ce{Fe2O3.xH2O}$ (Rust).
> 
> > [!picture]- Diagram of rust formation
> > ![[Pasted image 20260209100659.png]]

## Prevention of Corrosion

> [!liststart]- Method 1: Coating
> Coating the surface of metal to prevent water and air from contacting the metal (e.g., Paint).
> > [!continue]- Coating with Tin ($\ce{Sn}$)
> > * **Risk:** If the coating is scratched, tin will ||accelerate|| the corrosion of the exposed Iron.
> > * **Reason:**
> >     * $\ce{Sn^{2+} + 2e^{-} -> Sn} \quad (E^\circ = -0.14V)$
> >     * $\ce{Fe^{2+} + 2e^{-} -> Fe} \quad (E^\circ = -0.44V)$
> >     * Since $\ce{Sn}$ has a higher reduction potential, the oxidation of $\ce{Fe}$ is preferential.

> [!liststart]- Method 2: Cathodic Protection
> The method of using a metal (**sacrificial anode**) of a ||more negative|| ${E^{o}}_\text{red}$ to promote reduction of the protected metal.
> > [!example]- Galvanized Iron (Zinc Coating)
> > Coating $\ce{Fe}$ with $\ce{Zn}$.
> > * **Mechanism:** $\ce{Zn}$ acts as the sacrificial anode.
> >     * $\ce{Zn^{2+} + 2e^{-} -> Zn} \quad (E^\circ = -0.76V)$
> >     * $\ce{Fe^{2+} + 2e^{-} -> Fe} \quad (E^\circ = -0.44V)$
> > * **Result:** $\ce{Zn}$ is preferentially oxidized over $\ce{Fe}$ and provides electrons for the reduction of $\ce{Fe^{2+}}$ to $\ce{Fe}$.
>
> > [!example]- Underground Pipes
> > Connecting $\ce{Zn}$ blocks (sacrificial anode) by a wire to underground $\ce{Fe}$ water pipe.
> >  ![[Pasted image 20260209100833.png]]
>
> > [!example]- Aluminum Hulls (Boats)
> > $\ce{Mg}$ is connected by a wire to the hull.
> > * The $\ce{Mg}$ will preferentially oxidize and promotes the reduction of $\ce{Al^{3+}}$ to $\ce{Al(s)}$.

## Batteries (Cells)

> [!compare]- Primary vs Secondary Cells
> > [!continue]- Primary Cell
> > Discharges electrical current resulting from a chemical reaction but the reaction ||cannot be reversed||.
> > * Cell is **not** rechargeable.
> > * Examples: Dry cell, Alkaline battery, Lithium battery.
>
> > [!continue]- Secondary Cell
> > Capable of being ||reversed|| so that the $E^\circ$ potential is restored.
> > * Cell is **rechargeable**.
> > * Examples: Lead storage battery, Fuel cells.
